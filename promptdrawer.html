How to use this pack

Each prompt is built the same way: a line telling you when to use it, the full prompt text to copy, a list of the placeholders to fill in, and a tip for what to do with the answer.

  1. Find the prompt that fits the job you have right now, using the list below.
  2. Replace every [SQUARE BRACKET] with your own details. Delete placeholders that do not apply rather than leaving them in.
  3. Paste the whole thing into an AI assistant (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot — any of them).
  4. Read the answer critically. The prompts tell the assistant to work from what you gave it and to ask you questions instead of inventing facts, but you know your business best.
  5. If the answer is close but not right, use the follow-up line in the tip instead of starting again.

What is not claimed here

This is a set of written prompts: not a course, not software, not a subscription. It makes no promise about results, savings, revenue or time, because nobody has measured those for this pack. What is true is narrow and checkable — there are 50 prompts, each is paste-ready, every placeholder is marked and explained, and each carries a tip.

Marketing & Positioning

7 prompts

The jobs in this section decide whether people understand what you sell and why it matters to them. Everything here works from what you already know about your own business, so you can do it without paying for market research.

1. The One-Sentence Positioning Statement

When to use: You have to introduce yourself - in a bio, a directory listing, an email signature, a networking round - and what you write now either rambles or sounds like every other supplier in your trade.

Help me write a one-sentence positioning statement for my business. Here are the facts: What I sell, in plain words: [WHAT YOU SELL] Who buys it: [WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE] The one thing I do differently from others offering something similar: [YOUR DIFFERENCE] What the customer actually walks away with: [THE RESULT THEY GET] A sentence I already use, if I have one: [CURRENT SENTENCE, OR "none yet"] Write five one-sentence versions. Each must be under 20 words, use plain everyday language, and pass this test: a stranger reading only that sentence can tell who it is for and what they get. Vary the angle across the five: one aimed at who it is for, one at the outcome, one at the problem it removes, one at how I work, and one that says what I am not. Then, in the same reply: 1. List any words in my versions that are vague or that any competitor could also claim. 2. Say which single version you would put at the top of my website, and why. 3. Ask me the two questions you still need answered to make it sharper. Do not invent details about my business and do not claim results I have not told you about.
Fill in
  • [WHAT YOU SELL] — your service or product in the words a customer would use, not trade jargon.
  • [WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE] — the type of customer, and what stage they are at (for example "one-person trades businesses that just got their first employee").
  • [YOUR DIFFERENCE] — how you work or what you include that most others in your trade do not.
  • [THE RESULT THEY GET] — what is different for the customer afterwards.
  • [CURRENT SENTENCE, OR "none yet"] — what you say today so the assistant can see what to avoid repeating.

Tip: Read the five out loud to one person who does not know your business, then ask them what you sell. If they cannot answer, the sentence is not wrong, it is just carrying too much. Put the version that survives in your bio, your email signature and the top of your website, and keep the wording the same in all three places.

2. Who Is Actually Buying From Me

When to use: You are about to write a page, an ad or a follow-up email and you realise you cannot describe your buyer beyond "small businesses" or "people who need this".

Help me build a factual profile of who buys from me. Work only from the evidence I give you. My business: [WHAT YOU SELL] What I think my best customer looks like: [YOUR GUESS] My last five customers, in this format - who they were, what they bought, what they said their problem was, how they found me, what they paid: [PASTE THE FIVE, ONE PER LINE] Do this in three parts: Part 1 - What the evidence shows. For each of the five, write one line on what they had in common with the others. Then state the single smallest group that covers most of my real buyers - describe it in one sentence I could read out loud. Part 2 - What is still a guess. List everything I have assumed about my buyer that is not supported by the five examples. Be blunt. Put them in a bullet list headed "Assumptions I have not tested". Part 3 - The cheapest way to test each one. For each assumption, suggest one thing I can check this week without spending money - for example a question in a job enquiry, a look at my own past messages, or asking three past customers directly. If five examples are not enough to see a pattern, say so instead of inventing one.
Fill in
  • [WHAT YOU SELL] — one line describing your offer.
  • [YOUR GUESS] — who you currently picture when you think of a good customer.
  • [PASTE THE FIVE, ONE PER LINE] — your five most recent or most profitable customers, with the four details listed. Remove customer names if you prefer; their sector and size is what matters.

Tip: Keep Part 3. Put the assumptions you cannot test into a note and stop writing them as if they were facts on your website. When you next talk to a customer who went well, ask them the two questions from Part 3 rather than asking whether they are happy.

3. Price Against The Three Real Alternatives

When to use: You are setting a price, or thinking about raising one, and your only reference point is what other people in your trade charge - or what you charged last year.

Help me understand what my price is actually competing against. What I sell: [WHAT YOU SELL] My current or planned price: [PRICE, OR RANGE] Who buys it: [WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE] What it costs me to deliver: [YOUR ROUGH COST OR TIME, IN YOUR OWN WORDS] My customer has three realistic alternatives to paying me, and I want to take them seriously: - Doing nothing, or living with the problem - Doing it themselves, badly and slowly - Hiring someone else, listed here: [ANY COMPETITORS OR TYPES OF SUPPLIER YOU KNOW OF] Do this in three parts: Part 1 - A table with one row per alternative. Columns: Alternative, What it costs them, What it does not solve, Why someone still picks it. Fill it in from the information I gave you and mark any cell you are guessing with the word GUESS. Part 2 - Three price points for my offer: roughly 20 per cent below my current price, my current price, and roughly 20 per cent above. For each, write what a buyer would assume about the quality or the scope at that price, and one sentence of positioning that would make that price believable. Part 3 - Tell me which alternative I am losing most often to, based on the table, and the one thing I should change in how I describe my price to win against it. Do not research or estimate market prices you do not know; use only what I gave you.
Fill in
  • [WHAT YOU SELL] — the offer being priced.
  • [PRICE, OR RANGE] — what you charge now, or the range you are considering.
  • [WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE] — the buyer type.
  • [YOUR ROUGH COST OR TIME, IN YOUR OWN WORDS] — hours, materials, subcontractor cost - whatever makes up the cost of doing the job.
  • [ANY COMPETITORS OR TYPES OF SUPPLIER YOU KNOW OF] — a competitor, a big chain, a freelancer market place, a friend who "does it cheap" - name what is actually true where you work.

Tip: Copy Part 1 into your own notes and add to it every time a customer tells you why they hesitated. Within a few jobs the "Why someone still picks it" column becomes your most useful marketing asset, because it tells you which sentence on your website is doing the work.

4. Offer Teardown: Make It Easier To Say Yes To

When to use: People say your service sounds interesting, then go quiet after the quote. Or every enquiry ends with a request for a discount.

Act as a critical reviewer of my offer. Do this as a four-step chain and label your answer with the step numbers. Step 1 - Reasons to say no. Based only on what I write below, list the five most likely reasons a real buyer would say no or stall. Write them in the buyer's own voice, as sentences they would say out loud. Step 2 - Rank them. Put the five in order of how often each one probably happens, and explain your ranking in one line each. If two are too close to call, say so. Step 3 - Rewrite the offer. Rewrite my offer so the top three reasons no longer apply. You may only use: clearer scope, clearer price, a smaller first step, proof I already have, or removing something that creates doubt. You may not add guarantees, discounts, deadlines or urgency that I have not told you exist. Step 4 - What changed. Show the rewritten offer in full, then list in a table what you changed, why, and what I would have to be able to deliver for that change to be honest. My offer, as I would say it to a customer: [YOUR OFFER, WORD FOR WORD] What it costs: [PRICE] What I already know about why people hesitate: [ANYTHING YOU HAVE HEARD] What I can honestly promise: [WHAT YOU CAN ACTUALLY DELIVER, INCLUDING LIMITS]
Fill in
  • [YOUR OFFER, WORD FOR WORD] — write it as you say it out loud to a customer, not as it is polished on your website.
  • [PRICE] — the number you quote.
  • [ANYTHING YOU HAVE HEARD] — real hesitations you have heard, or write "nothing".
  • [WHAT YOU CAN ACTUALLY DELIVER, INCLUDING LIMITS] — this is what keeps Step 3 honest. Include what you will not do, how long things really take, and what you need from the customer.

Tip: Run Step 3 twice if the first rewrite feels like a different business. Send the rewritten offer to the next enquiry as a test: use it word for word and see which reasons from Step 1 still come up. The ones that survive are usually about price, and prompt 12 handles those.

5. The Questions They Will Ask Before They Buy

When to use: You keep rewriting your website or your quote letter and it never lands, because you are answering the questions you find interesting rather than the ones the buyer actually asks.

I want to know the questions a buyer has before they spend money with me, and whether I can answer them honestly. My business and offer: [WHAT YOU SELL AND WHAT IT COSTS] Who buys: [WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE] What I say about it now: [PASTE YOUR CURRENT DESCRIPTION OR QUOTE TEXT] Produce a table with these columns: 1. Question - written in the buyer's words, in the order they would think of it 2. What a good answer sounds like - one or two sentences I could actually say 3. Do I have a real answer? - mark YES, PARTLY or NO for each, using only what I told you 4. If it is PARTLY or NO - what I would have to change, add or find out before I can answer it Cover 10 to 12 questions. Include the uncomfortable ones: what it costs, how long it takes, what happens if it goes wrong, why you and not someone cheaper, and what I need from them. Then finish with two things: - The single question where changing my offer would help me more than writing a better answer. - The two questions I should put on my website, and where. Do not write invented proof, reviews or results into any answer.
Fill in
  • [WHAT YOU SELL AND WHAT IT COSTS] — the offer and the price.
  • [WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE] — the buyer.
  • [PASTE YOUR CURRENT DESCRIPTION OR QUOTE TEXT] — whatever you send or publish today.

Tip: Column 3 is the point of this prompt. Every NO is a question you are currently answering with hope. Fix the offer or find the information before you fix the wording, and put the two questions from the end of the answer into your enquiry reply template so you answer them before they are asked.

6. Where To Show Up With Two Hours A Week

When to use: You know you should be marketing yourself and you have no idea which of the twenty possible channels deserves the two hours you actually have.

Help me choose where to spend my marketing time, given real limits. My business: [WHAT YOU SELL] Who buys: [WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE] The two hours a week I really have: [WHICH TWO HOURS, E.A. FRIDAY 4-6PM] Work I am willing to do: [WHICH OF THESE YOU CAN STICK TO: writing, talking on video, talking on the phone, posting photos of finished work, emailing past customers, going to events in person] Work I will not do: [WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW YOU WON'T KEEP UP] Money available for marketing: [AMOUNT OR "none"] My strongest asset: [FOR EXAMPLE: 200 past customers in my phone book, a visible shop front, ten years of local contacts, a portfolio of finished jobs] Give me a table of five realistic channels, scored 1 to 5 on each of these, with a one-line reason per score: Is my buyer there, Can I do this in two hours a week, Does it cost money, Does it build up over time, Am I willing to keep going for three months. Then: 1. Name one primary channel and one secondary, and explain the choice in three sentences. 2. List what I should deliberately ignore this quarter and one sentence on why, so I stop feeling guilty about it. 3. Give me a four-week plan for the primary channel: one action per week, each doable inside 60 minutes, described step by step. 4. Tell me the honest downside of my primary channel - what it will not do for me. Do not recommend anything that needs a budget I said I do not have.
Fill in
  • [WHAT YOU SELL] and [WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE] — as before.
  • [WHICH TWO HOURS] — be specific; the plan depends on it.
  • [WHICH OF THESE YOU CAN STICK TO] and [WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW YOU WON'T KEEP UP] — this keeps the answer realistic. Trying to force a channel you hate is the usual reason marketing stops in week two.
  • [AMOUNT OR "none"] — your real budget.
  • [YOUR STRONGEST ASSET] — the list you already own: past customers, location, contacts, work samples.

Tip: Diarise the four weeks before you close the file. At the end of week four, use prompt 47 to review what happened against the plan instead of quietly abandoning it. If the primary channel is not working after four honest weeks, switch to the secondary rather than adding a third.

7. Turn What I Already Do Into A Productised Offer

When to use: Every job gets priced from scratch, every scope is slightly different, and you have no idea what to quote until you have spent half an hour working it out.

Help me turn work I already do into one repeatable package with a fixed scope and a clear price band. My last five jobs, in this format - what the customer asked for, what I actually did, roughly how long it took, what I charged, and what parts I had to improvise: [PASTE THE FIVE] Cost of my time per hour, or what I need each working day to earn: [YOUR NUMBER OR ESTIMATE] What I am not willing to include: [ANYTHING YOU ALWAYS END UP DOING FOR FREE AND RESENT] Do this in four parts: Part 1 - Find the repeatable core. From the five jobs, list what appeared in almost all of them. That is the package. If there is no common core, say so plainly. Part 2 - Define the package. Write it out as: what the customer gets, in five bullets or fewer; what is deliberately not included; what I need from the customer before I start; roughly how long it takes; and a price band with the low and high end justified by the hours and the improvisation you saw in my five jobs. Part 3 - The edges. Write the exact sentences I say when a customer asks for something just outside the package, when they ask for a discount, and when they ask for it faster. Keep each one under 25 words and do not make promises I have not given you. Part 4 - Migration. Tell me how to move my next three enquiries onto the package without confusing existing customers, and what to change first in how I describe my work. Do not add services I have not told you I already deliver.
Fill in
  • [PASTE THE FIVE] — five recent jobs with the five details. Rough numbers are fine; the pattern matters more than precision.
  • [YOUR NUMBER OR ESTIMATE] — your hourly cost or daily target. If you do not know it, do prompt 35 first and come back.
  • [ANYTHING YOU ALWAYS END UP DOING FOR FREE AND RESENT] — this becomes the "not included" list and is often the most valuable part of the answer.

Tip: Use Part 2 as your standard quote text for the next month and keep a tally of what you had to say no to. If the same out-of-scope request comes up three times, it belongs in the package next version and the price should move with it.

Sales & Follow-Up

7 prompts

Selling on your own is mostly answering messages quickly and following up on the ones that went quiet, without nagging anyone. The prompts here write those messages for you and keep the follow-up honest: no invented deadlines, no pressure you cannot back up.

8. Reply To A New Enquiry While It Is Still Warm

When to use: An enquiry just landed - a form, a text, a message - and you are mid-job with no head space to write a good reply.

Write my reply to a new enquiry. Keep it under 120 words, plain and human, no corporate padding. The enquiry, copied exactly: [PASTE THE ENQUIRY] What I sell and roughly what it costs: [YOUR OFFER AND PRICE RANGE] What I need to know before I can quote: [THE TWO OR THREE FACTS YOU ALWAYS NEED] My real availability: [EARLIEST DATE OR WINDOW YOU COULD ACTUALLY START] Anything about the job that worries me: [OR "nothing"] Give me four things: 1. The reply. It must: answer the question they actually asked, ask only the two or three facts I need (no questionnaire), state my real availability as a fact rather than a promise, and end with one clear next step. 2. A text-message version, under 45 words, for the same enquiry. 3. A list of anything in the enquiry that suggests I should decline or be careful - only if there is something real, otherwise say "nothing stands out". 4. Three questions I should ask myself before I quote this job, based on what they wrote. Do not invent availability, discounts, certifications or waiting times I did not give you.
Fill in
  • [PASTE THE ENQUIRY] — the message as received, including the messy parts. The wording tells the assistant a lot about the person.
  • [YOUR OFFER AND PRICE RANGE] — what you do and roughly what it costs, so the reply does not accidentally imply something cheaper.
  • [THE TWO OR THREE FACTS YOU ALWAYS NEED] — the details without which you cannot price the job.
  • [EARLIEST DATE OR WINDOW YOU COULD ACTUALLY START] — a real date. If you are unsure, say what you know.
  • [OR "nothing"] — gut feeling about the job.

Tip: Save the reply as a template with the two or three facts as blanks, so the next enquiry takes two minutes rather than twenty. Send the text version when the enquiry came by text - matching their channel gets answered more often than switching to email.

9. Qualify It Before You Spend An Hour On It

When to use: You keep writing quotes that go nowhere, or taking on jobs that turn out to be a bad fit halfway through.

Act as a sales filter for a one-person business. Work only from what I give you. My business and typical job value: [WHAT YOU SELL, USUAL PRICE RANGE] What a good job looks like for me: [THE JOBS YOU WANT MORE OF] What a bad job looks like: [THE JOBS YOU REGRET TAKING] The enquiry in the customer's own words: [PASTE IT] Produce a table with one row per thing they told me. Columns: - What they said - What it tells me - and mark GUESS if you are inferring rather than reading - What I should ask next to confirm it - Green flag, amber or red flag Then: 1. Give the enquiry a fit score out of 10 using only the evidence, and one sentence explaining the score. 2. Write the three questions I should send to fill the biggest gaps. They must be answerable in one line each by a busy person. 3. Tell me plainly whether this is worth an hour of my time, and what would have to be true for the answer to change. Do not fill gaps with assumptions about the customer's budget or intent.
Fill in
  • [WHAT YOU SELL, USUAL PRICE RANGE] — so the assistant can judge whether the job is in your range.
  • [THE JOBS YOU WANT MORE OF] and [THE JOBS YOU REGRET TAKING] — be honest, including the jobs you regret for reasons that are not about money.
  • [PASTE IT] — the full enquiry, not a summary.

Tip: After five enquiries, look at the scores against what actually happened. If your red flags never turn into real problems, you are filtering too aggressively; if you keep accepting amber jobs that go wrong, add whatever they had in common to your "bad job" list and run this prompt again.

10. The Three-Message Follow-Up That Is Not Nagging

When to use: You sent a quote or a proposal and heard nothing back. You know you should follow up and you also do not want to be that person.

Write a three-message follow-up for a quote that has gone quiet. Each message must be under 90 words, sound like a person, and add something new. No "just checking in", no fake deadlines, no "final notice", no pressure. What I quoted: [WHAT YOU QUOTED AND THE PRICE] When I sent it: [DATE OR "X DAYS AGO"] What they said, if anything: [PASTE REPLIES OR "nothing"] Anything that has genuinely changed since - my availability, a price change, a new option: [OR "nothing has changed"] Anything I can honestly offer them instead if the full job is too much: [SMALLER OPTION OR "nothing"] Give me: 1. Message 1, for about two days after the quote: short, useful, one question that is easy to answer. 2. Message 2, for about a week later: it must contain something new - a worked example, an option, a clarification - not a repeat of message 1. 3. Message 3, for about two weeks after that: it closes the file politely and gives them an easy, no-awkwardness way to say no. 4. For each message: the subject line if it is email, and what I should do if they do not reply. 5. One sentence per message explaining what makes it not pushy. Do not add deadlines, discounts or urgency I have not told you exist. If nothing has genuinely changed, say so and write the messages without pretending it has.
Fill in
  • [WHAT YOU QUOTED AND THE PRICE] — enough detail that the messages can reference the job.
  • [DATE OR "X DAYS AGO"] — so the timing lines up with reality.
  • [PASTE REPLIES OR "nothing"] — any partial reply you got, even "let me think about it".
  • [OR "nothing has changed"] and [SMALLER OPTION OR "nothing"] — be truthful here. If nothing has changed, the follow-up should still be fine: people are busy, not hostile.

Note: This follow-up is for someone who contacted you first. Do not use it to send unsolicited bulk email.

Tip: Diary messages 1 to 3 the moment you send a quote and stop thinking about it until each comes up. Most quotes that convert are decided by message 2, and most people never send it. If all three get no reply, move them to a list you look at once a quarter rather than chasing again.

11. The One-Page Proposal

When to use: Your quotes are long, your emails are long, and you suspect the customer skims and then does nothing.

Turn my notes into a one-page proposal. One page means one page: if it does not fit, cut something rather than shrinking the text. What they asked for, in their words: [PASTE THEIR REQUEST OR SUMMARY OF THE CALL] What I will do, step by step: [YOUR LIST OF STEPS, IN PLAIN TERMS] What it costs: [PRICE, AND WHAT THE PRICE INCLUDES] Timings I can honestly commit to: [DATES OR "I cannot commit to dates yet"] What I need from them to start: [DEPOSIT, INFO, ACCESS, DECISIONS] What is not included: [THE SCOPE EDGE YOU ALWAYS GET ASKED FOR] What happens if something changes mid-job: [HOW YOU HANDLE CHANGES] Produce the proposal in this order: what you asked for, what I will do, what it costs, what I need from you, what is not included, what happens next. Use short sentences and plain headings. Keep every promise traceable to something I told you - mark anything you had to phrase as a placeholder with [CONFIRM] so I fill it in before sending. Then give me two extra things: 1. Three lines I should delete if they are only there to make the proposal longer. 2. One question I should ask the customer before sending this, because it would change the scope or the price.
Fill in
  • [PASTE THEIR REQUEST OR SUMMARY OF THE CALL] — mirroring their words is what makes a proposal land.
  • [YOUR LIST OF STEPS, IN PLAIN TERMS] — what actually happens, in order.
  • [PRICE, AND WHAT THE PRICE INCLUDES] — one number, with inclusions named.
  • [DATES OR "I cannot commit to dates yet"] — do not let the assistant invent a schedule you have no track record for.
  • [DEPOSIT, INFO, ACCESS, DECISIONS] — what has to happen on their side.
  • [THE SCOPE EDGE YOU ALWAYS GET ASKED FOR] — a line here prevents most disputes.
  • [HOW YOU HANDLE CHANGES] — your real policy on changes and extra costs.

Tip: Keep one master copy and change only the four job-specific blocks each time. Re-read it as if you were the customer: if you cannot find the price and the next step in ten seconds, cut something else.

12. Answer "That's Too Expensive" Without Cutting Your Price

When to use: The price lands and the conversation stalls. Your instinct is to knock something off, and you know that usually cheapens the job.

Help me respond to a price objection without discounting on the spot. What I quoted: [WHAT YOU QUOTED, WHAT IT INCLUDES, AND THE PRICE] What they said, exactly: [PASTE THEIR WORDS] What I know about their situation: [ANY REAL CONTEXT - TIMING, BUDGET THEY MENTIONED, WHAT THEY COMPARED IT TO] What my actual cost of delivering this job is: [YOUR COST OR TIME] What I can reduce, if anything, without making the job worse: [OR "nothing"] Give me: 1. Four different replies, each under 100 words, each using a different move: - acknowledge the concern without apologising for the price - compare the price to what the alternative really costs them - reduce the scope instead of the price - ask what they are comparing it to 2. The three questions that tell me whether this is really about money, or about trust, timing or uncertainty. Include what each answer would mean. 3. A "do not say" list of five phrases that make a one-person business sound desperate or make the price look negotiable, with a better alternative for each. 4. What to do if the honest answer is that they cannot afford it right now. Do not suggest fake scarcity, invented discounts, or implying the price ends soon.
Fill in
  • [WHAT YOU QUOTED, WHAT IT INCLUDES, AND THE PRICE] — so the replies can refer to the scope rather than arguing about a number.
  • [PASTE THEIR WORDS] — "too expensive compared to what?" is often answered in their own sentence.
  • [ANY REAL CONTEXT] — any clue about their budget or what they compared it to.
  • [YOUR COST OR TIME] — this is what tells you how much room you actually have.
  • [OR "nothing"] — if nothing can be cut without harm, say so and the assistant should work with that.

Tip: Pick the reply that feels most like you and edit it until you could say it on the phone without reading. The strongest long-run response to a price objection is usually the scope reduction, because it keeps the price intact and gives the customer a real choice.

13. Wake Up A Deal That Went Cold

When to use: Somebody enquired in spring, it drifted, and you know there is still a real job there - you just never found a reason to make contact again.

Write a one-off message to restart a conversation that went quiet. What they wanted: [WHAT THEY ASKED ABOUT] When we last spoke and what happened: [DATE AND WHAT WAS SAID] What I quoted, if anything: [PRICE OR "no quote sent"] What has genuinely changed since then: [FOR EXAMPLE: my availability, a change in my pricing, a seasonal window, a new option - or "nothing has changed"] How cold this is: [JUST WENT QUIET / A FEW WEEKS / MONTHS AGO / MORE THAN A YEAR] Give me three versions, each under 110 words: 1. Tip: Run this on three cold deals in one sitting, send the messages, and put a note in your diary for two weeks later. Whatever comes back is usually not a sale on the first message - it is permission to have the conversation again.

14. Turn One Finished Job Into The Next Three

  • [WHAT YOU DID] and [SCOPE AND PRICE] — the job, plainly.
  • [CUSTOMER TYPE AND ANYTHING YOU LEARNED ABOUT THEM] — their situation, so the next-work suggestion is real.
  • [PASTE OR PARAPHRASE THEIR FEEDBACK] — their words give the assistant the tone that works with this person.
  • [OR "nothing soon"] — if there is genuinely nothing to sell them, the answer should focus on review and introductions only.

Content & Social

7 prompts

Posting while running a business only works if it takes minutes, sounds like you, and does not require inventing anything. Every prompt here starts from something that already happened in your business - a customer question, a finished job, a complaint you solved.

My business and who it serves: [WHAT YOU SELL AND WHO BUYS] The question a customer asked me recently, in their words: [THE QUESTION AND YOUR ANSWER] What I can talk about confidently: [YOUR TWO OR THREE AREAS OF REAL EXPERIENCE] Anything I must not say: [PRICING DETAILS, CLIENT NAMES, LEGAL OR CONFIDENTIAL LIMITS, OR "nothing"] Produce a table with one row per post and these columns: Day, The angle, The first line (hook), The point in one sentence, What form it takes (short text post, photo of my work, list, short video, reply to a common myth), and What the reader should do next. Every post must trace back to that one customer question - the direct answer, the mistake that causes the question, the cost of getting it wrong, what most people assume, what to check first. Then finish with: 1. The two posts to write first, and why those two. 2. One post idea you deliberately left out because it would require information I did not give you.
Fill in
  • [WHAT YOU SELL AND WHO BUYS] — one line each.
  • [THE QUESTION AND YOUR ANSWER] — copy the actual message and your reply. This is the whole engine of the prompt.
  • [YOUR TWO OR THREE AREAS OF REAL EXPERIENCE] — so the posts stay in territory where you can answer follow-up comments.
  • [PRICING DETAILS, CLIENT NAMES, LEGAL OR CONFIDENTIAL LIMITS, OR "nothing"] — anything that cannot leave the building.

Tip: Write the two posts the assistant recommends, publish them, then run the same prompt next month with a different question. Keep the table in a note; when you are stuck later, you already have eight untouched angles. Replying to comments on these posts will usually teach you the next question to run through this prompt.

16. Make It Sound Like Me, Not Like A Robot

When to use: You ask an AI to write a post and what comes back is technically fine, and completely unlike how you talk.

How I talk, copied from messages I actually sent - three or four examples, unedited: [PASTE YOUR OWN WRITING] My draft: [PASTE YOUR DRAFT] Who is reading it: [YOUR CUSTOMERS, IN ONE LINE] What I want the reader to do after reading: [THE ONE ACTION] Do this: 1. List what is distinctive in my examples - sentence length, words I repeat, how I open and close, whether I use humour, how direct I am. Five bullets, quoted from my own text, no generalities. 2. Rewrite the draft at the same length or shorter, using what you found. Keep every fact from the draft. Remove anything that sounds like a brochure: stacked adjectives, "in today's fast-paced world", "we pride ourselves on", "solutions". 3. Show me a table: Original sentence, Rewritten sentence, Why it is closer to my voice. 4. Flag any sentence in my draft that I should probably delete rather than rewrite, because it is trying to do too much. 5. Tell me one habit in my writing you had to fight against, so I can watch for it myself. If my examples are too short to find a voice, say so and ask for more before rewriting.
Fill in
  • [PASTE YOUR OWN WRITING] — real messages you sent to customers, not your website copy. The unedited ones are what carry your voice.
  • [PASTE YOUR DRAFT] — the post, email or page you want fixed.
  • [YOUR CUSTOMERS, IN ONE LINE] — who reads it.
  • [THE ONE ACTION] — reply, book, call, buy, or just understand something.

Tip: Save the five bullets from part 1 as a note called "how I sound" and paste them at the top of future AI requests. It saves running this whole prompt every time. If part 5 keeps coming back with the same habit, that is a real editing weakness worth fixing yourself.

17. Four Posts In One Sitting, Once A Week

When to use: You post enthusiastically for three days and then disappear for a month, because posting daily does not survive contact with a busy week.

Set up a one-sitting weekly posting routine for me and produce this week's four posts. My business: [WHAT YOU SELL] Who reads my posts: [WHO THEY ARE AND WHAT THEY WORRY ABOUT] Where I post: [PLATFORM OR PLATFORMS] What happened in my business this week, in note form - jobs, questions, mistakes, decisions, small wins: [YOUR NOTES, MESSY IS FINE] How long I have: [E.G. 45 MINUTES ON FRIDAY AFTERNOON] Tone I am comfortable with: [E.G. PRACTICAL AND DRY / WARM AND INFORMAL / NO JOKES] Do this in four steps, labelled: Step 1 - Sort my notes. Put each item into one of: useful to a customer, interesting only to me, cannot be shared. Say what to do with the third pile. Step 2 - Four posts. Two from the "useful" pile, one that answers a question I get asked, and one that shows a decision or a mistake and what I learned. For each: the first line, the body under 120 words, and the closing question. No hashtag stuffing, no calls to action that sound like a marketing funnel. Step 3 - The order and spacing. Tell me which day to post each one and why that order. Step 4 - The routine. Write the steps I follow each week in this same sitting, so next week I only have to paste my notes. Include how I capture notes during the week and where the draft file should live. Do not invent customer stories, numbers or results I did not give you.
Fill in
  • [WHAT YOU SELL] and [WHO THEY ARE AND WHAT THEY WORRY ABOUT] — the audience decides the angles.
  • [PLATFORM OR PLATFORMS] — one or two, not all of them.
  • [YOUR NOTES, MESSY IS FINE] — a week of small observations. Half-formed is fine; this is where most of the value is.
  • [E.G. 45 MINUTES ON FRIDAY AFTERNOON] — the honest time budget. A routine that needs 90 minutes will not last.
  • [TONE I AM COMFORTABLE WITH] — so you will actually publish what comes back.

Tip: Run Step 4 once, keep the routine, and paste the same prompt with fresh notes each week. If four posts a week is still too many, change the number in step 2 to two rather than skipping weeks - a steady two beats a burst of eight followed by silence.

18. Turn A Job You Just Did Into A Story Without Exaggerating

When to use: You want to show your work - a before-and-after, a case story, a portfolio entry - and you are not willing to invent a number or a glowing testimonial to make it interesting.

Write a short story about work I just finished. Everything in it must be something I have given you. Where a detail would make the story better but I have not supplied it, leave a clearly marked gap instead of filling it in. What the job was: [WHAT YOU DID] Who it was for: [DESCRIBE THE CUSTOMER WITHOUT NAMING THEM, IF YOU PREFER] What the problem actually was: [WHAT WAS WRONG OR NEEDED] What I did, in order: [YOUR STEPS] What the result was, in facts I can state: [WHAT IS TRUE - E.G. "the leak stopped", "they passed the inspection", "it now takes 10 minutes a week"] What I cannot claim: [WHAT YOU CANNOT PROVE - E.G. NO FIGURES ON THEIR SALES OR SAVINGS] Permission and privacy: [WHETHER THE CUSTOMER AGREED TO BE MENTIONED, OR "I will keep them anonymous"] Give me: 1. The story, 200 to 300 words, in three parts: the situation, what was actually difficult, what I did and what is true about the outcome. 2. A version under 60 words for social media. 3. A list of every detail you left as a marked gap, in square brackets, with a question telling me exactly what I need to supply. 4. Any sentence in your draft that would count as an unverifiable claim if I published it as written, and a factual alternative that says the same thing honestly. Do not add numbers, savings, percentages, timelines or customer praise that I have not given you.
Fill in
  • [WHAT YOU DID] and [WHAT WAS WRONG OR NEEDED] — the plain facts.
  • [YOUR STEPS] — the order matters; that is the part customers cannot see and find most reassuring.
  • `[WHAT IS TRUE — ...]` - statements you would be comfortable repeating to the customer's face.
  • `[WHAT YOU CANNOT PROVE — ...]` - naming this in the prompt is what stops the output drifting into invented results.
  • [WHETHER THE CUSTOMER AGREED TO BE MENTIONED, OR "I will keep them anonymous"] — this is a real decision, not a stylistic one.

Tip: Publish the 60-word version first; it is easier to get right and easier to redo. Keep the 200-word version for your website or a quote attachment, where a story about the actual difficulty of the job does more work than another list of features. If the customer has not agreed to be named, keep them anonymous by role - "a three-person plumbing firm" - and check before using photographs.

19. Answer A Comment Or Message That Needs Care

When to use: Someone has posted under your content or messaged you in a way that makes you want to either delete it, argue, or defend yourself.

Help me reply to a public comment or message. I want to be accurate, calm and finished with it, not to win an argument. The comment, copied exactly: [PASTE IT] What is actually true: [THE FACTS, INCLUDING WHERE I WAS WRONG IF I WAS] What I know about the person: [CUSTOMER, WOULD-BE CUSTOMER, COMPETITOR, STRANGER, OR "no idea"] Anything I cannot legally or contractually discuss: [OR "nothing"] What I want to happen next: [E.G. TAKE IT OFFLINE, CORRECT THE RECORD, APOLOGISE PROPERLY, LET IT GO] Give me four options: 1. A brief public reply, under 60 words, for when I was not at fault but want to end the exchange. 2. A longer public reply for when I was partly or fully wrong, which acknowledges the specific thing, states what I am doing about it, and does not blame the customer. 3. A private follow-up if the matter should move out of public view. 4. A "no reply" option, with a note on when saying nothing is the correct choice. Fill in
  • [PASTE IT] — exactly, including spelling and tone. Rewriting it in your own words hides the cues.
  • [THE FACTS, INCLUDING WHERE I WAS WRONG IF I WAS] — being straight here changes the quality of the output completely.
  • [CUSTOMER, WOULD-BE CUSTOMER, COMPETITOR, STRANGER, OR "no idea"] — a competitor baiting you and a disappointed customer need different replies.
  • [OR "nothing"] — anything sensitive or contractual.
  • [E.G. TAKE IT OFFLINE, ...] — what a good outcome looks like for you.

Tip: Screenshot the comment, reply once, and stop. If it is a paying customer with a real complaint, switch to prompt 22 and solve the problem privately. If you find yourself drafting a fourth version, the correct answer is usually the "no reply" option or a two-line version of option 1.

20. Rewrite My Draft So It Actually Gets Read

When to use: You have written an email, a page or a post, you know it is too long, and you cannot see what to cut because you wrote it.

Edit my draft hard. I care about being read, not about keeping every sentence. My draft: [PASTE THE FULL DRAFT] Who is reading it and what they already know: [YOUR READER] What I need them to do or understand: [THE ONE OUTCOME] Where it will be read: [EMAIL, WEB PAGE, SOCIAL POST, PRINTED QUOTE, WINDOW NOTICE] Do this in one pass, as five labelled parts: 1. One sentence: what my draft is actually about, as you read it. If you cannot tell, say "unclear" and name the two competing topics. 2. A table: Original sentence, Editorial note (keep / cut / move / rewrite), Reason in six words or fewer. Cover every sentence. 3. The rewritten version, 40 per cent shorter or more, with the action or main point in the first two sentences. 4. The three things I lost in the rewrite that I might care about, so I can overrule you. 5. One question I should answer in the draft and have not, based on what a reader in this position would need to know. Do not add facts, promises, statistics or deadlines that are not already in my draft.
Fill in
  • [PASTE THE FULL DRAFT] — including the messy version. Do not pre-tidy it; the mess is the diagnosis.
  • [YOUR READER] — and what they know already, so the rewrite does not over-explain.
  • [THE ONE OUTCOME] — what should change after they read it.
  • [EMAIL, WEB PAGE, SOCIAL POST, PRINTED QUOTE, WINDOW NOTICE] — the format changes the rewrite substantially.

Tip: Take part 2 seriously and do the cuts yourself once, on a piece that matters. After three or four passes you will start catching the same three bad habits before the draft is even finished, which is faster than running this prompt every time. Part 3 is a draft, not a verdict - read it out loud before you send it.

21. A Newsletter Issue In Ten Minutes

When to use: You have a list of past customers, you know it is worth emailing them, and the idea of writing to all of them at once freezes you.

Write this month's email to my list. It should be useful enough to open and small enough that I will actually send it. Who is on my list: [PAST CUSTOMERS, PROSPECTS, OR BOTH, AND ROUGHLY HOW MANY] What I do: [WHAT YOU SELL] One thing worth telling them this month - a seasonal reminder, something I have learned, a change in how I work, an answer to a question I keep getting: [THE ONE THING] Anything I want to offer, only if it is real and available now: [OR "nothing this month"] How often I intend to send this: [MONTHLY, QUARTERLY] How people signed up: [WHETHER THEY OPTED IN, OR "it is a list of past customers"] Give me: 1. Three subject lines, honest and specific, under nine words each. No clickbait, no fake urgency, no re-opened loops. 2. The email, under 200 words: one idea, why it matters to them, one action, and a closing line that does not beg. 3. A one-line version I could send as a text message or a quick message instead of an email. 4. The unsubscribe and consent line to include, and one sentence telling me whether this list can be emailed at all given how those people signed up. 5. Fill in
  • [PAST CUSTOMERS, PROSPECTS, OR BOTH, AND ROUGHLY HOW MANY] — the audience and the size.
  • [THE ONE THING] — a single idea. Two ideas is how newsletters turn into unread catalogues.
  • [OR "nothing this month"] — honest option. If there is no offer, the email should be useful anyway.
  • [WHETHER THEY OPTED IN, OR "it is a list of past customers"] — this matters for point 4. Only email people who agreed to hear from you, include a way to opt out, and never use this prompt to write cold bulk email.

Tip: Keep a running note titled "next issue" all month and drop in one line whenever you think of something. The email takes ten minutes to assemble when the idea is already captured. Send to past customers who opted in, keep the promise of your stated frequency, and one useful email a month will beat a swallowed quarterly newsletter every time.

Customer Support & Retention

6 prompts

For a one-person business, support is not a department - it is you, mid-job, with a message you have to answer well. These prompts cover the hard conversations: complaints, impossible requests, quiet dissatisfaction, and the policies you have been explaining verbally for years.

22. Answer A Complaint Before It Escalates

When to use: A customer is unhappy and you can feel yourself either wanting to argue or wanting to over-promise to make it stop.

FIX IT AND KEEP THE CUSTOMER, END THE RELATIONSHIP CLEANLY] Give me: 1. A reply under 180 words that: acknowledges the specific problem without a generic apology, states what happens next with a real time frame, and does not admit or deny liability beyond what I have told you is true. 2. Two things I should not write in this reply, and what to write instead. 5. A one-line note on what to record about this for my own records, in case it comes up again. Fill in
  • [THE FACTS I HAVE] — what actually happened, including the parts that are your fault.
  • [PASTE THEIR MESSAGE] — exactly as sent.
  • `[YOUR REAL OPTIONS — ...]` - list them all, including "do not compensate". The assistant works within your options, so give it real ones.
  • `[CONSTRAINTS — TIME, COST, CONTRACT, SAFETY]` - what rules out easy answers.
  • [E.G. FIX IT AND KEEP THE CUSTOMER, END THE RELATIONSHIP CLEANLY] — your actual goal.

Tip: Send part 1 within the hour if you can, even if the fix takes a week - an acknowledged problem is easier to live with than silence. Then set a reminder to check in when you said you would, because most escalations come from the second broken promise, not the first mistake. Logging part 5 takes ten seconds and settles arguments months later.

What they asked for: [PASTE THE REQUEST] Why I am saying no: [THE REAL REASON - CAPACITY, SCOPE, PRICE, SAFETY, ETHICS, TERMS] What I can offer instead, if anything: [SMALLER JOB, LATER DATE, DIFFERENT APPROACH, OR "nothing"] How important this customer is to me: [CRITICAL, IMPORTANT, MARGINAL, ONE-OFF] How I have handled similar requests before: [OR "this is the first time"] Give me three versions of the same no: 1. Short and warm, under 60 words: no plus one useful alternative. 2. Full and clear, under 140 words: the no, the reason stated in one sentence without over-explaining, what I can do instead, and what happens next. 3. The repeat version, for when they ask the same thing a second or third time: polite, shorter, and unmistakably final. For each version, add a line telling me what I am risking by sending it.   • [PASTE THE REQUEST] — their words.
  • `[THE REAL REASON — ...]` - you can decide whether to state it plainly or give a shorter version; the assistant needs the truth to write either.
  • [SMALLER JOB, LATER DATE, DIFFERENT APPROACH, OR "nothing"] — a no with an alternative usually lands better, so list anything you would genuinely do.
  • [CRITICAL, IMPORTANT, MARGINAL, ONE-OFF] — this changes the tone a lot.
  • [OR "this is the first time"] — patterns matter more than single events.

Tip: Pick version 2 the first time and version 3 if the request comes back, and use the same wording each time rather than improvising a new softer answer. If you find yourself saying no to the same customer every month, that is a scope conversation, not a series of refusals - prompt 7 is the better tool.

24. Check On A Customer Who Has Gone Quiet

When to use: A regular customer has not been in touch for longer than usual, and you would rather find out why now than discover the relationship ended six months ago.

A short check-in message under 100 words that asks about their situation first and does not mention money, prices or "just following up". 2. A version to use if I know something went wrong: it should name the issue directly and invite them to tell me what happened. 3. A short set of three questions to ask them if they reply, designed to reveal whether this is a service problem, a budget problem, a change in circumstance, or nothing at all. 4. A note on what I should do differently if several quiet customers turn out to have the same reason. Do not offer discounts, compensation or special deals unless I listed them, and do not imply that I am owed their business.
Fill in
  • [CUSTOMER AND USUAL WORK] — their pattern tells you what "gone quiet" even means.
  • [HOW LONG, AND WHEN THEY LAST SPENT] — the gap.
  • `[ANY REAL CONTEXT — ...]` - if they changed jobs or the season ended, the message changes completely.
  • [OR "nothing I know of"] — be honest; if something went wrong, directness works much better than a cheerful check-in.
  • [E.G. REWORK, A CALL, AN EXPLANATION, NOTHING IF I WAS RIGHT] — what you would actually do if they told you they were unhappy.

Tip: Run this on three quiet customers at once, once a quarter, and keep a note of the reasons. If two of them give the same reason, that is a fix worth making in your process rather than a message worth sending again. Remember these are people you have traded with, not a mailing list to work through.

25. Write My Rules In Plain English

When to use: You have explained the same refund rule, cancellation policy or scope limit four times this year, slightly differently each time, and you would like one version you can actually send.

Turn my business rules into plain-English wording I can use everywhere. My business: [WHAT YOU SELL] The rules I actually follow, told plainly, even the awkward ones: [LIST EVERYTHING - DEPOSITS, CANCELLATION, REFUNDS, LATE PAYMENT, SCOPE CHANGES, WHO OWNS THE WORK, WHAT YOU NEED FROM THE CUSTOMER] How I explain them today: [PASTE ANY EXISTING TEXT, OR "I improvise each time"] When they apply: [AT QUOTING, AT BOOKING, AFTER DELIVERY] Rules I am unsure about or want an opinion on: [OR "none"] Anything a contract or a professional body requires me to state: [OR "nothing"] Give me: 1. A table of the rules that are unclear, contradictory, or that I would struggle to enforce, with a question I need to answer for each. 4. Fill in
  • `[LIST EVERYTHING — ...]` - the real rules, as you actually apply them, including the ones you have never written down.
  • [PASTE ANY EXISTING TEXT, OR "I improvise each time"] — so the rewrite keeps anything that already works.
  • [AT QUOTING, AT BOOKING, AFTER DELIVERY] — the moment matters; a refund rule told at quoting prevents a fight later.
  • [OR "none"] and [OR "nothing"] — honest options.
  • [OR "none"] for rules you are unsure about — these are usually the ones causing your disputes.

Tip: Put the plain-English numbered version in your quote template so customers see the rules before they pay, and keep the table from part 3 for yourself. When a dispute comes up, quote your own wording rather than inventing a new explanation under pressure. Where a rule touches money, ownership or liability, have someone qualified read it before it goes on a contract.

A list of the invoices or receipts I should chase now, in the order that has the most effect, with the dates I should chase them. 4. What would have to happen for this to become a real problem, described in plain terms - for example a customer paying a month late, or a job not coming off. 5. Fill in
  • [INVOICES DUE WITH DATES, REGULAR WORK, OR "I do not know"] — even a guess marked as a guess is more useful than nothing.
  • [CUSTOMER, AMOUNT, DATE DUE] — your own unpaid invoices, with how overdue they are.
  • [LIST WITH DATES OR DAYS OF THE MONTH] — when money actually leaves, so the tight week is visible.
  • [TAX, INSURANCE, EQUIPMENT, RENT REVIEW, OR "none"] — the irregular hits are what catch people out.
  • [AMOUNT, FREQUENCY, OR "none"] — finance and loan payments.
  • [AMOUNT], [AMOUNT AND HOW OFTEN] — the starting point and what you take out.
  • [YOUR WORRY, OR "nothing specific"] — naming it means the answer can address it.

Tip: Update this once a month with the same prompt and keep the previous versions; the pattern across three months is far more useful than any single one. If step 4 shows a real risk, run prompt 49 to plan for it before it happens, and start the chasing from step 3 immediately - the oldest invoice is almost always the hardest to collect.

LOSING THEM, BEING RUDE, GOING TOO SOFT] Give me: 1. Message 1, first reminder: short, assumes it slipped, restates the facts and asks for a payment date. Under 80 words. 2. Message 2, a week later: firmer, states the consequence that is actually true and that I can carry out (for example pausing further work or charging agreed late fees only if they exist), and asks for a specific date. 3.   • [CUSTOMER TYPE AND RELATIONSHIP] — repeat customer, one-off, or someone you want to keep.
  • [THE WORK] — what was delivered, so the messages can reference it factually.
  • [AMOUNT AND REFERENCE] — exact figures. Vagueness weakens every message.
  • [DUE DATE] and [DAYS OR WEEKS] — the timeline is your strongest fact.
  • [OR "nothing yet"] — the history of chasing.
  • [OR "nothing"] — verbal arrangements people often "remember" differently.
  • [E.G. 14 DAYS, OR "I did not state terms"] — honest answer here leads to sound advice in point 5.
  • [E.G. LOSING THEM, BEING RUDE, GOING TOO SOFT] — what you want to protect.

Tip: Send message 1 the day you notice the invoice is late rather than at the end of the month, and keep all three in one thread so there is a dated record. Put the sentence from point 6 on every future invoice - clear terms at the start prevent most of this. If the amount is large or the customer disputes it, take advice before the final notice.

40. Read My Numbers Back To Me In Plain English

Period covered: [E.G. LAST THREE MONTHS, OR LAST MONTH] My figures, roughly or exactly, however you have them: [INCOME, EXPENSES, CASH IN THE BANK, INVOICES NOT YET PAID, BEST AND WORST MONTH, ANYTHING ELSE YOU HAVE] What was unusual in this period: [EG. A BIG JOB, A QUIET MONTH, A ONE-OFF PURCHASE] What I already think is going on: [YOUR READ] What I am trying to decide: [E.G. WHETHER TO HIRE, PUT PRICES UP, CUT SOMETHING, TAKE A BREAK] Give me: 1. Five sentences a friend would understand: what came in, what went out, what is left, what is owed to me, what I owe. Use plain words, not accounting terms. 2. What changed since the previous period, and whether the change is big enough to be meaningful or just a normal wobble. 3. The three things these numbers genuinely do not tell you - the questions I cannot answer from the figures I gave you. 4. Fill in
  • [E.G. LAST THREE MONTHS, OR LAST MONTH] — the period, so changes can be compared.
  • [INCOME, EXPENSES, CASH IN THE BANK, INVOICES NOT YET PAID, BEST AND WORST MONTH, ...] — whatever you have. Rough figures give a rough read, and that is stated in the answer.
  • [EG. A BIG JOB, A QUIET MONTH, A ONE-OFF PURCHASE] — so a one-off is not read as a trend.

  • [E.G. WHETHER TO HIRE, PUT PRICES UP, CUT SOMETHING, TAKE A BREAK] — the decision you are heading towards.

Tip: Do this monthly, five minutes with the same three figures, and keep the five sentences from part 1 in one note - reading twelve of them in a row tells you more about your business than any single report. Where part 3 names a question you cannot answer, that is usually the next thing to track. Anything about tax, debt or contracts goes to someone qualified, with these figures in hand.

Hiring & Delegating

5 prompts

The first person you bring in, or the first freelancer you use, changes how the business works. These prompts are about being specific: what the job actually is, what a good answer sounds like, and what finished means so it does not come back to you.

41. The Job Post For The Person I Actually Need

When to use: You are hiring or taking on a freelancer, and your last attempt produced a hundred applications, none of whom could do the job.

Write a job post for the person I actually need, and be honest about the work. The tasks I want off my plate, described as they happen day to day: [LIST THE ACTUAL TASKS, INCLUDING THE BORING ONES] The tasks I will keep doing myself: [WHAT YOU ARE NOT HANDING OVER] What has to be true from day one: [E.G. DRIVING LICENCE, OWN TOOLS, WEEKEND AVAILABILITY, SPECIFIC SOFTWARE, LANGUAGE] What can be learned on the job: [SKILLS YOU ARE WILLING TO TRAIN] What the arrangement is: [FULL TIME, PART TIME, CASUAL HOURS, FREELANCE, PROJECT BY PROJECT] What I can pay: [AMOUNT OR RANGE, AND HOW OFTEN] Where the work happens: [ON SITE, REMOTE, ON THE ROAD, MIXED] Who they report to and how: [YOU, AND THE CHANNEL - PHONE, MESSAGE, IN PERSON] What previous attempts got wrong: [EG. PEOPLE WHO WANTED A DESK JOB, PEOPLE WITH NO RELIABILITY, APPLICANTS WHO ASKED FOR MORE MONEY] How I will decide: [EG. SHORT MESSAGE, PHONE CALL, TRIAL DAY, TRIAL JOB] Give me: 1. The job post, organised as: what the work actually is (a list of tasks in ordinary language), what the week looks like, what I need you to have, what I will teach you, what the arrangement and pay are, and how to apply. 2. Three lines to include that would put off the wrong applicant without misleading anyone, and three lines that would wrongly put off a good one, which I should avoid. 3. A version for a noticeboard or social post, under 120 words, for when I am not posting on a job site. 4. The two facts about this role that are least attractive, and the honest sentence to include about each - so nobody arrives expecting something else. Do not invent qualifications, benefits, perks or a salary figure I did not give you. Do not use words that promise career development, training or a supportive culture unless I said those things exist.
Fill in
  • [LIST THE ACTUAL TASKS, INCLUDING THE BORING ONES] — the day-to-day reality, not the job title.
  • [WHAT YOU ARE NOT HANDING OVER] — sets an honest boundary from the start.
  • [E.G. DRIVING LICENCE, OWN TOOLS, ...] — genuine requirements only.
  • [SKILLS YOU ARE WILLING TO TRAIN] — widens the pool and prevents "must have 5 years experience" postings you cannot justify.
  • [FULL TIME, PART TIME, CASUAL HOURS, FREELANCE, PROJECT BY PROJECT] — the arrangement.
  • [AMOUNT OR RANGE, AND HOW OFTEN] — pay. Check what a fair rate is for your area and comply with local employment rules.
  • [ON SITE, REMOTE, ON THE ROAD, MIXED] — where and when.
  • `[YOU, AND THE CHANNEL — ...]` - who they deal with.
  • [EG. PEOPLE WHO WANTED A DESK JOB, ...] — your past failures are the most useful part of this prompt.
  • [EG. SHORT MESSAGE, PHONE CALL, TRIAL DAY, TRIAL JOB] — how you will choose, and be prepared to pay for trial work.

Tip: Post it, then read your first three applications against part 4 - if nobody is put off by the worst parts of the role, the post is still selling a job that does not exist. For anything beyond a casual arrangement, check your local employment obligations before you offer, since pay, hours and notice rules are not things an AI should be deciding.

42. Questions That Separate Talkers From Doers

When to use: You are about to interview, you are not confident at interviewing, and the last person you hired interviewed better than they worked.

Write my interview questions for a practical role, and tell me what to listen for. The role and the tasks that matter most: [WHAT THEY WILL ACTUALLY DO] The two things that would make me let someone go: [EG. UNRELIABILITY, RUSHING, POOR COMMUNICATION] The level of experience I am hiring at: [TRAINEE, EXPERIENCED, FREELANCER] The tools, licences or background checks involved: [OR "none"] How long the interview is: [MINUTES] Who else is in the room, if anyone: [OR "just me"] What I am allowed to ask where I am: [ANY LOCAL LIMITS YOU KNOW OF, OR "I do not know"] Give me: 1. Six questions. Each must ask about something the person has actually done or would do next week - no hypotheticals about strengths and weaknesses, no questions that invite a rehearsed speech. 2. For each question, in a table: What it is really testing, What a strong answer includes (concrete detail, not adjectives), What a weak answer sounds like, and One follow-up question to catch a vague answer. 3. A practical task or a short paid trial that would tell me more than the interview, how long it should take, and what to look for. 4. Three questions I should not ask, with the reason, and a fair alternative for each. 5. A note on what to write down during the interview so I can compare candidates afterwards without relying on my memory. 6. One reference-check question that goes beyond "would you hire them again". Do not write questions about age, family, health, religion, nationality or anything similar. If you are unsure whether something is legal to ask where I am, flag it and tell me to check.
Fill in
  • [WHAT THEY WILL ACTUALLY DO] — the tasks decide the questions.
  • [EG. UNRELIABILITY, RUSHING, POOR COMMUNICATION] — interview for the failure you have already experienced.
  • [TRAINEE, EXPERIENCED, FREELANCER] — the level changes what counts as a good answer.
  • [OR "none"] — licences and checks you need to cover in the interview process.
  • [MINUTES] — so the number of questions is realistic.
  • [OR "just me"] — affects your note-taking.
  • [ANY LOCAL LIMITS YOU KNOW OF, OR "I do not know"] — point 4 depends on this. If you do not know, say so and check your local rules before interviewing.

Tip: Score each candidate on the part 2 table straight after the interview, before you look at the next one, and always do the paid trial from part 3 if the role allows it - a two-hour paid task tells you more than an hour of conversation. Keep the notes; comparing four sets of scribbles from memory never works.

43. Hand It Over So It Does Not Come Back

When to use: You are giving a task to someone for the first time, and your history is that you end up redoing it yourself because it came back wrong or half-finished.

Help me hand this task over properly, the first time. The task: [WHAT IT IS] Who I am handing it to: [PERSON, THEIR EXPERIENCE, WHAT THEY ALREADY KNOW] Why I am handing it over: [SO I GET TIME BACK, SO IT GETS DONE BETTER, SO IT IS NOT JUST ME] What "finished and good" looks like: [DESCRIBE THE OUTCOME, INCLUDING THE STANDARD - NOT JUST "DONE"] What they must not do: [HARD LIMITS - SPENDING, PROMISES TO CUSTOMERS, CHANGING PRICES, CONTACTING CERTAIN PEOPLE] What they can decide alone: [DECISIONS THEY CAN MAKE WITHOUT ASKING] When to come to me: [THE CONDITIONS THAT SHOULD STOP THEM AND BRING IT TO ME] Deadline or frequency: [WHEN IT IS DUE, OR HOW OFTEN IT HAPPENS] Tools, logins or information they need: [OR "none"] How long the handover can take: [MINUTES AVAILABLE TO EXPLAIN IT] Give me: 1. The handover brief: what the task is, what finished looks like, the limits, what they can decide, when to ask me, and how it fits into everything else. One page, plain language, no corporate filler. 2. The three questions I should invite them to ask me before starting, because those are where the mistakes will come from. 3. A short checklist they use to know they are finished, written for them, not for me. 4. What to check the first two times they do it, and what to stop checking after that. 5. The one sentence that tells them what to do if they cannot finish in the time available - so late work arrives early instead of silently slipping. Fill in
  • [PERSON, THEIR EXPERIENCE, WHAT THEY ALREADY KNOW] — the brief should avoid explaining what they already know.
  • `[DESCRIBE THE OUTCOME, INCLUDING THE STANDARD — NOT JUST "DONE"]` - the standard is what usually goes missing and causes the rework.
  • `[HARD LIMITS — ...]` - spending limits, promises they cannot make, information they should not share.
  • [DECISIONS THEY CAN MAKE WITHOUT ASKING] — being clear about this is what stops constant interruptions.
  • [THE CONDITIONS THAT SHOULD STOP THEM AND BRING IT TO ME] — define the trigger, not the feeling.
  • [MINUTES AVAILABLE TO EXPLAIN IT] — keeps the brief to a length you will actually deliver.
  • [OR "none"] — access they need.

Tip: Walk through the brief once in person or on a call, then send it in writing, and ask them to repeat back what finished looks like in their own words. The first time it comes back, resist the urge to redo it - use prompt 45 instead, or the same mistake will return and you will end up doing it yourself permanently.

44. The First Week For Someone New

When to use: Someone new starts soon, and you have no plan beyond showing them where things are - which means their first week will be your least productive week.

The role and what they will mainly do: [ROLE AND MAIN TASKS] Where they are starting from: [EXPERIENCE LEVEL AND WHAT THEY NEED TO BE TOLD] How many hours in the first week: [HOURS AND DAYS] How much of my time is available in that week: [REALISTIC HOURS, BEING HONEST ABOUT MY OWN WORKLOAD] What they need access to or to be told: [TOOLS, KEYS, LOGINS, SAFETY, CUSTOMER CONTACTS, MONEY HANDLING] The first piece of real work they could do on their own: [THE SIMPLEST REAL JOB] Mistakes I need to prevent in week one: [OR "nothing specific"] Who else they will deal with: [CUSTOMERS, SUPPLIERS, OTHER TEAM MEMBERS] Give me: 1. A day-by-day plan for the five days, with what I do, what they do, and what they read or watch. Keep my time commitment inside the hours I said I have. 2. A one-page "how this business works" note to hand them on day one: who we serve, how work arrives, what good looks like, what to do when unsure. 3. A short list of what to show them by doing it in front of them rather than explaining, and why each one. 4. The first task to hand over, with what finished looks like, and my check points. 5. Two questions to ask at the end of the week that tell me whether the plan worked, and what to change for the next person. 6. A list of things I should prepare before they arrive, with realistic time estimates. Do not invent policies, benefits or training I have not described. Where something must be true for legal or safety reasons, ask me rather than assuming.
Fill in
  • [ROLE AND MAIN TASKS] — what the week is building towards.
  • [EXPERIENCE LEVEL AND WHAT THEY NEED TO BE TOLD] — so the plan does not over- or under-explain.
  • [HOURS AND DAYS] — their first-week hours, likely shorter than normal.
  • [REALISTIC HOURS, BEING HONEST ABOUT MY OWN WORKLOAD] — the usual failure is a plan that assumes you have a spare day.
  • [TOOLS, KEYS, LOGINS, SAFETY, CUSTOMER CONTACTS, MONEY HANDLING] — access and responsibility, decided in advance.
  • [THE SIMPLEST REAL JOB] — real work beats training exercises.
  • [OR "nothing specific"] — the mistake you most want to prevent.
  • [CUSTOMERS, SUPPLIERS, OTHER TEAM MEMBERS] — who they will meet.

Tip: Prepare part 6 the week before they start, when you are not also answering their questions, and keep the day-by-day plan visible for both of you - it stops the first week collapsing into shadowing. Anything to do with pay, contracts, safety or insurance needs checking locally before day one, not in the middle of it.

45. Feedback That Actually Changes Something

When to use: Something came back wrong, late or not to standard, and either you say nothing and quietly fix it, or you say something and it lands badly.

Help me give feedback about a specific piece of work, so the next attempt is better rather than the person feeling attacked. Who it is for and how we work together: [PERSON, EMPLOYEE OR FREELANCER, HOW LONG, HOW OFTEN THEY WORK WITH YOU] What was expected: [THE OUTCOME AND THE STANDARD] What happened instead: [THE FACTS - WHAT ARRIVED, WHEN, WHAT WAS WRONG, WHAT THE CONSEQUENCE WAS] What was genuinely outside their control: [OR "nothing"] Whether I told them clearly beforehand: [YES, NO, OR PARTLY - BE HONEST] Whether this is the first time or a pattern: [FIRST TIME, SECOND, HAPPENS OFTEN] What I want to be different next time: [THE SPECIFIC CHANGE] How I would prefer to deliver it: [IN PERSON, ON A CALL, IN WRITING] Give me: 1. The feedback in four sentences or fewer: what was expected, what happened, the effect, and what to do differently next time. No adjectives about the person, only specifics about the work. 2. Three short follow-up lines for the likely responses: agreement, disagreement, and a reason I had not considered. Include what I say if they disagree and they are right. 3. The version for a pattern rather than a one-off, which must state clearly that this is a recurring issue and what happens if it continues - using only consequences I have told you are real. 4. A version for a freelancer I will not use again, or will pause, that stays factual and pays for work already done. 5. What to write down afterwards, and what to say next time this person does it well. 6. One sentence about me: what I could have made clearer at the start, if my answer to whether I explained it was "no" or "partly". Fill in
  • [THE OUTCOME AND THE STANDARD] — what you actually asked for, and how clearly.
  • `[THE FACTS — ...]` - what arrived, when, and what it cost you. Consequence makes feedback land.
  • [OR "nothing"] — anything genuinely outside their control, which should be removed from the feedback.
  • `[YES, NO, OR PARTLY — BE HONEST]` - this is what makes point 6 worth reading.
  • [FIRST TIME, SECOND, HAPPENS OFTEN] — repetition changes the wording entirely.
  • [THE SPECIFIC CHANGE] — what to do differently, stated so it can be acted on.
  • [IN PERSON, ON A CALL, IN WRITING] — your preference, since how it is delivered matters as much as what is said.

Tip: Give the feedback within a day or two, use the four-sentence version even if you wrote a longer one, and then follow point 5 by noticing the next time they get it right - unacknowledged good work is how the same problem comes back. If the issue is a pattern, write the date and the summary down, because a one-line record made at the time is worth more than a remembered conversation.

Getting Unstuck

5 prompts

Every operator has a decision they keep putting off, a project that is too big to start, and a month they would rather not look at. These five prompts are for those moments. They are written to give you a next step, not a pep talk.

46. The Decision I Keep Putting Off

When to use: A decision has been sitting in your head for weeks - a price rise, a supplier, a customer you should probably drop, whether to keep going with something - and thinking about it has become a habit.

Help me make a decision I have been avoiding. Do not decide for me - build the decision so I can decide in ten minutes. The decision, phrased as a question with two or more options: [E.G. "DO I RAISE PRICES 15% OR KEEP THEM AND TAKE MORE WORK?"] How long I have been putting it off: [WEEKS OR MONTHS] Why I have not decided: [YOUR HONEST REASON - FEAR OF LOSING CUSTOMERS, NO TIME, NOT ENOUGH INFORMATION, SOMEONE ELSE'S OPINION] The facts I am sure of: [LIST] What I do not know: [LIST] What happens if I do nothing for another three months: [YOUR BEST GUESS] Who else is affected: [OR "just me"] What I would tell a friend in this position: [YOUR ANSWER, OR "I do not know"] The deadline, real or self-imposed, if there is one: [OR "none"] Give me: 1. The decision restated in one sentence, with the options listed. Include "do nothing" as an explicit option with its cost. 2. A table: Option, What has to be true for it to be the right choice, What it costs me if I am wrong, How reversible it is, What I would need to know to choose it. 3. The two options that actually survive scrutiny, and why the others are weaker - be willing to say an option is weak. 4. The reversible parts: what I could try on a small scale before committing fully, and what that test would cost. 5. The information that would change the decision, and whether it is worth getting versus just deciding now with what I have. 6. One question to answer before deciding, and what my answer would tell me. Where my facts are thin, ask me for the missing figure rather than assuming it.
Fill in
  • [E.G. "DO I RAISE PRICES 15% OR KEEP THEM AND TAKE MORE WORK?"] — the decision as a question with real options. Vague framing produces vague output.
  • [WEEKS OR MONTHS] — how long it has been sitting there.
  • `[YOUR HONEST REASON — ...]` - the real reason, not the polite one. It usually points at which risk matters most.
  • [LIST] and [LIST] — what you know and what you do not. Writing the second list is often the decision.
  • [YOUR BEST GUESS] — the cost of drift, which is the option people forget.
  • [OR "just me"] — who else is affected, including family or a business partner.
  • [YOUR ANSWER, OR "I do not know"] — your own instinct, so the answer can confirm or challenge it.
  • [OR "none"] — real or self-imposed deadlines; both are useful to name.

Tip: Decide within twenty-four hours of running this, because the value is in choosing, not in having the perfect answer. If the reversible test in point 4 is cheap, do that instead of deciding at all - a small trial beats another month of thinking. Write your chosen option and the date in a note so that in three months you can check whether it was right.

47. Where Did The Week Actually Go

When to use: It is Friday, you have worked all week, and you could not say what you got done or whether it moved anything forward.

Review my week against what I planned, honestly and without judgement. Facts first, then one change. What I planned for this week: [PASTE YOUR PLAN OR LIST WHAT YOU INTENDED] What I actually did, hour by hour where I can remember and roughly where I cannot: [YOUR RECORD - MESSY IS FINE] Money in and out this week: [AMOUNTS OR "no idea"] Enquiries, quotes and follow-ups: [NUMBERS OR "I did not track"] What got pushed to next week, and how many weeks it has been pushed: [LIST] What went wrong or surprised me: [OR "nothing"] What I avoided: [OR "nothing"] Time I actually worked: [ROUGH HOURS] Give me: 1. A comparison table: Planned, Did, Moved the business forward (yes / no / unclear), and the reason. 2. Where my week actually went, in four or five categories with rough hours, based only on what I told you. Mark anything you inferred as GUESS. 3. The three things I did that will not matter in a month - said plainly, not unkindly. 4. What got pushed again, and the one decision I should make about each rather than pushing it a fourth time. 5. My honest score for the week in one line, and the single most valuable thing I did - which may not be the one that felt productive. 6. One change for next week only. Not a system, not a habit, one change, with the exact thing to do differently. 7. One question to ask myself at the start of next week so this review takes ten minutes instead of thirty. Do not tell me to be more disciplined. Do not invent numbers I did not give you.
Fill in
  • [PASTE YOUR PLAN OR LIST WHAT YOU INTENDED] — the plan you wrote on Monday, not the one you remember. This is why prompt 28 is worth keeping.
  • `[YOUR RECORD — MESSY IS FINE]` - a rough diary, calendar entries, or a list from memory. Accuracy matters more than completeness.
  • [AMOUNTS OR "no idea"], [NUMBERS OR "I did not track"] — honesty here is the point; "no idea" is itself useful information.
  • [LIST] — pushed tasks and how often they have been pushed.
  • [OR "nothing"] — what surprised you, and what you avoided. The avoided item is usually the important one.
  • [ROUGH HOURS] — the hours you actually worked, not the hours you felt busy.

Tip: Run this every Friday in the same ten-minute slot and keep the output, so in six weeks you can read them in one sitting and see patterns no single week shows. Take point 6 seriously and change exactly one thing - five changes at once is how the review itself gets abandoned. If the same task has been pushed four times, that is prompt 46, not a time-management problem.

48. Turn This Overwhelming Project Into This Week

When to use: Something big needs doing - a new website, quoting system, product, tax catch-up, clearing out a mess - and it has been "starting next week" for two months because it is too large to face.

Help me start a project I have been avoiding and make a real first week out of it. What the project is, as I understand it: [DESCRIBE IT BADLY - THAT IS FINE] Why it matters: [WHAT CHANGES IF IT IS DONE] Why I have not started: [HONEST REASON - TOO BIG, NO TIME, DO NOT KNOW WHERE TO BEGIN, DO NOT WANT TO] What already exists: [ANY PROGRESS, FILES, NOTES, OR "nothing"] The deadline, if there is a real one: [OR "none"] Hours per week I can give it: [REALISTIC - E.G. THREE ONE-HOUR BLOCKS] What I need it to be good enough to do: [THE MINIMUM USEFUL OUTCOME, NOT THE DREAM VERSION] Anything outside my control: [SUPPLIERS, OTHER PEOPLE, SOFTWARE, APPROVALS] Give me: 1. The project split into stages, each with a clear finish line, in the order they must happen. Note which stage is the one that usually stops people. 2. The smallest first version that would be genuinely useful, and what to leave out of it on purpose. 3. The next five actions, each doable in under sixty minutes, written as physical actions starting with a verb. Each one must be something I can do alone, today or this week. 4. A plan for the first week using my real available hours, and what I will not do that week instead. 5. The two decisions I have to make before starting, and what I would need to know to make each. If a decision can be made either way without much cost, say so - I am not allowed to stall on reversible choices. 6. What to tell other people, if anything, so the project is not quietly sabotaged by normal life. Do not give me a timeline in months, or a plan that requires work I cannot do alone.
Fill in
  • `[DESCRIBE IT BADLY — THAT IS FINE]` - an unclear description is enough to start; the first action is often clarifying it.
  • [WHAT CHANGES IF IT IS DONE] — the reason to push through the middle part.
  • `[HONEST REASON — ...]` - the real blocker, so the plan can work around it.
  • [ANY PROGRESS, FILES, NOTES, OR "nothing"] — so you do not restart from zero.
  • [OR "none"] — a real deadline changes the plan; a self-imposed one can move.
  • `[REALISTIC — E.G. THREE ONE-HOUR BLOCKS]` - hours like this beat "I will do a bit every day".
  • [THE MINIMUM USEFUL OUTCOME, NOT THE DREAM VERSION] — this is what makes it finishable.
  • [SUPPLIERS, OTHER PEOPLE, SOFTWARE, APPROVALS] — so you know what to start early.

Tip: Do action one from point 3 within the next hour, before you plan anything else, and give the project a fixed slot in the week rather than finding time for it. Finish a stage before improving the previous one, and if you have not touched it for two weeks, come back to point 3 and pick the smallest action on the list instead of replanning from the top.

49. Plan For The Month That Goes Badly

When to use: You are nervous about the next few months - a quiet season, a big customer leaving, a cost going up - and "I will deal with it if it happens" is not really a plan.

Help me plan for a bad month before it happens. I want decisions made now so I am not making them under pressure. My business and how it earns: [WHAT YOU SELL AND WHO PAYS YOU] My average monthly income over the last six months: [AMOUNT OR ROUGH RANGE] The bad scenario I am worried about: [EG. INCOME DROPS 30% FOR TWO MONTHS, A BIG CUSTOMER LEAVES, A KEY COST RISES] My regular costs, split into what I must pay and what I could pause: [TWO LISTS] Money I have set aside for exactly this: [AMOUNT OR "nothing"] Commitments I cannot break: [CONTRACTS, LEASES, STAFF, LOANS, OR "none"] What I could sell or start quickly if I needed income: [YOUR IDEAS] Who would be affected by my decisions: [FAMILY, STAFF, SUPPLIERS, OR "just me"] Give me: 1. Trigger points: how I will know the bad month has started rather than just being a slow week. Give me three concrete triggers I can see in my own numbers, not a feeling. 2. A prepared decision list, in order, in a table: Trigger reached, The decision already made, Who I tell and when, What it costs, How reversible it is. 3. The first three cuts or changes, ranked by how fast they take effect, and the exact step to make each one. 4. What I do first to bring income in, using only the things I said I could start quickly, and how it fits around existing work. 5. What I should NOT do, and why - including the tempting moves that make a bad month worse. 6. One thing to prepare while things are fine: the document or list to have ready so the plan takes an hour to run, not a week. 7. A note on anything in my plan that depends on someone else agreeing, since that is where the timing will slip. Do not recommend borrowing, selling assets, or taking on debt. If the plan looks like it will not be enough, say so plainly and tell me what that means.
Fill in
  • [WHAT YOU SELL AND WHO PAYS YOU] — the revenue shape.
  • [AMOUNT OR ROUGH RANGE] — the normal baseline, so a "30 per cent drop" can be made concrete.
  • [EG. INCOME DROPS 30% ...] — name the specific scenario. A vague fear produces a vague plan.
  • [TWO LISTS] — what must be paid versus what could be paused. This distinction does most of the work.
  • [AMOUNT OR "nothing"] — your buffer, honestly.
  • [CONTRACTS, LEASES, STAFF, LOANS, OR "none"] — what cannot be cut.
  • [YOUR IDEAS] — income options you would actually consider.
  • [FAMILY, STAFF, SUPPLIERS, OR "just me"] — the plan affects people, and that shapes what you prepare.

Tip: Prepare point 6 now, save it somewhere you will find it in a bad week, and read it once a quarter so it stays current. When a trigger is reached, run the decision list in order rather than inventing a new plan at 11pm. Anything involving contracts, debt or employment obligations needs professional advice, and getting it before the trigger is far cheaper than getting it after.

50. Argue Against My Own Plan

When to use: You have a plan, an idea, a launch or a big decision you feel good about - and nobody around you is going to test it properly.

Be the strongest honest critic of my plan. Your job is to find the weakest parts, not to encourage me. The plan, in full: [DESCRIBE IT - WHAT I WILL DO, FOR WHOM, OVER WHAT PERIOD] What has to be true for it to work: [YOUR ASSUMPTIONS, STATED AS ASSUMPTIONS] My evidence for each assumption: [WHAT YOU KNOW VERSUS WHAT YOU BELIEVE] What it costs me: [TIME, MONEY, OPPORTUNITY] What I would stop doing to make room for it: [OR "nothing"] What success looks like at 30, 90 and 180 days, in numbers I can actually check: [YOUR FIGURES] Who benefits if I am right: [WHO PAYS YOU AND WHY THEY WOULD] What I have already spent or committed: [OR "nothing"] How I will know if it is failing: [YOUR ANSWER, OR "I have not decided"] Do this in five parts: 1. The five weakest assumptions, ranked by how much damage it does if the assumption is wrong. Mark each one: could I test it cheaply, or am I betting on it? 2. The single thing most likely to kill this plan, and the early warning sign I would see first. 3. A pre-mortem: it is nine months from now and this failed. Write three short, plausible stories of how, told in the past tense. 4. What my plan looks like to someone who is not being kind - including whether it is a plan or a wish, and what would move it from one to the other. 5. The three changes that would make the plan considerably more robust, each one sentence, and one thing I should keep exactly as it is because it is genuinely sound. Use only what I gave you. Do not invent market data, competitor behaviour or statistics, and do not soften your criticism to be polite. If the plan is sound, say so, but only after looking hard.
Fill in
  • `[DESCRIBE IT — WHAT I WILL DO, FOR WHOM, OVER WHAT PERIOD]` - enough detail to be argued with.
  • [YOUR ASSUMPTIONS, STATED AS ASSUMPTIONS] — listing them yourself is half the exercise.
  • [WHAT YOU KNOW VERSUS WHAT YOU BELIEVE] — this distinction is what part 1 ranks on.
  • [TIME, MONEY, OPPORTUNITY] — all three costs, including what else you could do with the time.
  • [OR "nothing"] — what you will stop to make room.
  • [YOUR FIGURES] — checkable numbers, not adjectives.
  • [WHO PAYS YOU AND WHY THEY WOULD] — if you cannot answer this, that is the finding.
  • [OR "nothing"] — money or time already committed.
  • [YOUR ANSWER, OR "I have not decided"] — how you will spot failure, which is often missing from plans.

Tip: Run this before you spend money, and once more halfway through - the second run usually exposes a different weakness, because by then you know things you did not know at the start. Decide in advance what early warning sign would make you stop, write it down, and then answer part 4 honestly. If the plan survives this without changes, you either wrote it well or asked too gently; try prompt 47 four weeks in, which tests the plan against what actually happened.
