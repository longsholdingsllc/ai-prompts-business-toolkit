# Marketing & Positioning

The jobs in this section decide whether people understand what you sell and why it matters to them. Everything here works from what you already know about your own business, so you can do it without paying for market research.

## 1. The One-Sentence Positioning Statement

**When to use:** You have to introduce yourself - in a bio, a directory listing, an email signature, a networking round - and what you write now either rambles or sounds like every other supplier in your trade.

**Prompt:**

```text
Help me write a one-sentence positioning statement for my business.

Here are the facts:

What I sell, in plain words: [WHAT YOU SELL]
Who buys it: [WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE]
The one thing I do differently from others offering something similar: [YOUR DIFFERENCE]
What the customer actually walks away with: [THE RESULT THEY GET]
A sentence I already use, if I have one: [CURRENT SENTENCE, OR "none yet"]

Write five one-sentence versions. Each must be under 20 words, use plain everyday language, and pass this test: a stranger reading only that sentence can tell who it is for and what they get.

Vary the angle across the five: one aimed at who it is for, one at the outcome, one at the problem it removes, one at how I work, and one that says what I am not.

Then, in the same reply:
1. List any words in my versions that are vague or that any competitor could also claim.
2. Say which single version you would put at the top of my website, and why.
3. Ask me the two questions you still need answered to make it sharper.

Do not invent details about my business and do not claim results I have not told you about.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[WHAT YOU SELL]` - your service or product in the words a customer would use, not trade jargon.
- `[WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE]` - the type of customer, and what stage they are at (for example "one-person trades businesses that just got their first employee").
- `[YOUR DIFFERENCE]` - how you work or what you include that most others in your trade do not.
- `[THE RESULT THEY GET]` - what is different for the customer afterwards.
- `[CURRENT SENTENCE, OR "none yet"]` - what you say today so the assistant can see what to avoid repeating.

**Tip:** Read the five out loud to one person who does not know your business, then ask them what you sell. If they cannot answer, the sentence is not wrong, it is just carrying too much. Put the version that survives in your bio, your email signature and the top of your website, and keep the wording the same in all three places.

## 2. Who Is Actually Buying From Me

**When to use:** You are about to write a page, an ad or a follow-up email and you realise you cannot describe your buyer beyond "small businesses" or "people who need this".

**Prompt:**

```text
Help me build a factual profile of who buys from me. Work only from the evidence I give you.

My business: [WHAT YOU SELL]
What I think my best customer looks like: [YOUR GUESS]
My last five customers, in this format - who they were, what they bought, what they said their problem was, how they found me, what they paid: [PASTE THE FIVE, ONE PER LINE]

Do this in three parts:

Part 1 - What the evidence shows. For each of the five, write one line on what they had in common with the others. Then state the single smallest group that covers most of my real buyers - describe it in one sentence I could read out loud.

Part 2 - What is still a guess. List everything I have assumed about my buyer that is not supported by the five examples. Be blunt. Put them in a bullet list headed "Assumptions I have not tested".

Part 3 - The cheapest way to test each one. For each assumption, suggest one thing I can check this week without spending money - for example a question in a job enquiry, a look at my own past messages, or asking three past customers directly.

If five examples are not enough to see a pattern, say so instead of inventing one.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[WHAT YOU SELL]` - one line describing your offer.
- `[YOUR GUESS]` - who you currently picture when you think of a good customer.
- `[PASTE THE FIVE, ONE PER LINE]` - your five most recent or most profitable customers, with the four details listed. Remove customer names if you prefer; their sector and size is what matters.

**Tip:** Keep Part 3. Put the assumptions you cannot test into a note and stop writing them as if they were facts on your website. When you next talk to a customer who went well, ask them the two questions from Part 3 rather than asking whether they are happy.

## 3. Price Against The Three Real Alternatives

**When to use:** You are setting a price, or thinking about raising one, and your only reference point is what other people in your trade charge - or what you charged last year.

**Prompt:**

```text
Help me understand what my price is actually competing against.

What I sell: [WHAT YOU SELL]
My current or planned price: [PRICE, OR RANGE]
Who buys it: [WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE]
What it costs me to deliver: [YOUR ROUGH COST OR TIME, IN YOUR OWN WORDS]

My customer has three realistic alternatives to paying me, and I want to take them seriously:
- Doing nothing, or living with the problem
- Doing it themselves, badly and slowly
- Hiring someone else, listed here: [ANY COMPETITORS OR TYPES OF SUPPLIER YOU KNOW OF]

Do this in three parts:

Part 1 - A table with one row per alternative. Columns: Alternative, What it costs them, What it does not solve, Why someone still picks it. Fill it in from the information I gave you and mark any cell you are guessing with the word GUESS.

Part 2 - Three price points for my offer: roughly 20 per cent below my current price, my current price, and roughly 20 per cent above. For each, write what a buyer would assume about the quality or the scope at that price, and one sentence of positioning that would make that price believable.

Part 3 - Tell me which alternative I am losing most often to, based on the table, and the one thing I should change in how I describe my price to win against it.

Do not research or estimate market prices you do not know; use only what I gave you.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[WHAT YOU SELL]` - the offer being priced.
- `[PRICE, OR RANGE]` - what you charge now, or the range you are considering.
- `[WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE]` - the buyer type.
- `[YOUR ROUGH COST OR TIME, IN YOUR OWN WORDS]` - hours, materials, subcontractor cost - whatever makes up the cost of doing the job.
- `[ANY COMPETITORS OR TYPES OF SUPPLIER YOU KNOW OF]` - a competitor, a big chain, a freelancer market place, a friend who "does it cheap" - name what is actually true where you work.

**Tip:** Copy Part 1 into your own notes and add to it every time a customer tells you why they hesitated. Within a few jobs the "Why someone still picks it" column becomes your most useful marketing asset, because it tells you which sentence on your website is doing the work.

## 4. Offer Teardown: Make It Easier To Say Yes To

**When to use:** People say your service sounds interesting, then go quiet after the quote. Or every enquiry ends with a request for a discount.

**Prompt:**

```text
Act as a critical reviewer of my offer. Do this as a four-step chain and label your answer with the step numbers.

Step 1 - Reasons to say no. Based only on what I write below, list the five most likely reasons a real buyer would say no or stall. Write them in the buyer's own voice, as sentences they would say out loud.

Step 2 - Rank them. Put the five in order of how often each one probably happens, and explain your ranking in one line each. If two are too close to call, say so.

Step 3 - Rewrite the offer. Rewrite my offer so the top three reasons no longer apply. You may only use: clearer scope, clearer price, a smaller first step, proof I already have, or removing something that creates doubt. You may not add guarantees, discounts, deadlines or urgency that I have not told you exist.

Step 4 - What changed. Show the rewritten offer in full, then list in a table what you changed, why, and what I would have to be able to deliver for that change to be honest.

My offer, as I would say it to a customer:
[YOUR OFFER, WORD FOR WORD]
What it costs: [PRICE]
What I already know about why people hesitate: [ANYTHING YOU HAVE HEARD]
What I can honestly promise: [WHAT YOU CAN ACTUALLY DELIVER, INCLUDING LIMITS]
```

**Fill in:**

- `[YOUR OFFER, WORD FOR WORD]` - write it as you say it out loud to a customer, not as it is polished on your website.
- `[PRICE]` - the number you quote.
- `[ANYTHING YOU HAVE HEARD]` - real hesitations you have heard, or write "nothing".
- `[WHAT YOU CAN ACTUALLY DELIVER, INCLUDING LIMITS]` - this is what keeps Step 3 honest. Include what you will not do, how long things really take, and what you need from the customer.

**Tip:** Run Step 3 twice if the first rewrite feels like a different business. Send the rewritten offer to the next enquiry as a test: use it word for word and see which reasons from Step 1 still come up. The ones that survive are usually about price, and prompt 12 handles those.

## 5. The Questions They Will Ask Before They Buy

**When to use:** You keep rewriting your website or your quote letter and it never lands, because you are answering the questions you find interesting rather than the ones the buyer actually asks.

**Prompt:**

```text
I want to know the questions a buyer has before they spend money with me, and whether I can answer them honestly.

My business and offer: [WHAT YOU SELL AND WHAT IT COSTS]
Who buys: [WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE]
What I say about it now: [PASTE YOUR CURRENT DESCRIPTION OR QUOTE TEXT]

Produce a table with these columns:
1. Question - written in the buyer's words, in the order they would think of it
2. What a good answer sounds like - one or two sentences I could actually say
3. Do I have a real answer? - mark YES, PARTLY or NO for each, using only what I told you
4. If it is PARTLY or NO - what I would have to change, add or find out before I can answer it

Cover 10 to 12 questions. Include the uncomfortable ones: what it costs, how long it takes, what happens if it goes wrong, why you and not someone cheaper, and what I need from them.

Then finish with two things:
- The single question where changing my offer would help me more than writing a better answer.
- The two questions I should put on my website, and where.

Do not write invented proof, reviews or results into any answer.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[WHAT YOU SELL AND WHAT IT COSTS]` - the offer and the price.
- `[WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE]` - the buyer.
- `[PASTE YOUR CURRENT DESCRIPTION OR QUOTE TEXT]` - whatever you send or publish today.

**Tip:** Column 3 is the point of this prompt. Every NO is a question you are currently answering with hope. Fix the offer or find the information before you fix the wording, and put the two questions from the end of the answer into your enquiry reply template so you answer them before they are asked.

## 6. Where To Show Up With Two Hours A Week

**When to use:** You know you should be marketing yourself and you have no idea which of the twenty possible channels deserves the two hours you actually have.

**Prompt:**

```text
Help me choose where to spend my marketing time, given real limits.

My business: [WHAT YOU SELL]
Who buys: [WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE]
The two hours a week I really have: [WHICH TWO HOURS, E.A. FRIDAY 4-6PM]
Work I am willing to do: [WHICH OF THESE YOU CAN STICK TO: writing, talking on video, talking on the phone, posting photos of finished work, emailing past customers, going to events in person]
Work I will not do: [WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW YOU WON'T KEEP UP]
Money available for marketing: [AMOUNT OR "none"]
My strongest asset: [FOR EXAMPLE: 200 past customers in my phone book, a visible shop front, ten years of local contacts, a portfolio of finished jobs]

Give me a table of five realistic channels, scored 1 to 5 on each of these, with a one-line reason per score: Is my buyer there, Can I do this in two hours a week, Does it cost money, Does it build up over time, Am I willing to keep going for three months.

Then:
1. Name one primary channel and one secondary, and explain the choice in three sentences.
2. List what I should deliberately ignore this quarter and one sentence on why, so I stop feeling guilty about it.
3. Give me a four-week plan for the primary channel: one action per week, each doable inside 60 minutes, described step by step.
4. Tell me the honest downside of my primary channel - what it will not do for me.

Do not recommend anything that needs a budget I said I do not have.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[WHAT YOU SELL]` and `[WHO YOUR CUSTOMERS ARE]` - as before.
- `[WHICH TWO HOURS]` - be specific; the plan depends on it.
- `[WHICH OF THESE YOU CAN STICK TO]` and `[WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW YOU WON'T KEEP UP]` - this keeps the answer realistic. Trying to force a channel you hate is the usual reason marketing stops in week two.
- `[AMOUNT OR "none"]` - your real budget.
- `[YOUR STRONGEST ASSET]` - the list you already own: past customers, location, contacts, work samples.

**Tip:** Diarise the four weeks before you close the file. At the end of week four, use prompt 47 to review what happened against the plan instead of quietly abandoning it. If the primary channel is not working after four honest weeks, switch to the secondary rather than adding a third.

## 7. Turn What I Already Do Into A Productised Offer

**When to use:** Every job gets priced from scratch, every scope is slightly different, and you have no idea what to quote until you have spent half an hour working it out.

**Prompt:**

```text
Help me turn work I already do into one repeatable package with a fixed scope and a clear price band.

My last five jobs, in this format - what the customer asked for, what I actually did, roughly how long it took, what I charged, and what parts I had to improvise: [PASTE THE FIVE]
Cost of my time per hour, or what I need each working day to earn: [YOUR NUMBER OR ESTIMATE]
What I am not willing to include: [ANYTHING YOU ALWAYS END UP DOING FOR FREE AND RESENT]

Do this in four parts:

Part 1 - Find the repeatable core. From the five jobs, list what appeared in almost all of them. That is the package. If there is no common core, say so plainly.

Part 2 - Define the package. Write it out as: what the customer gets, in five bullets or fewer; what is deliberately not included; what I need from the customer before I start; roughly how long it takes; and a price band with the low and high end justified by the hours and the improvisation you saw in my five jobs.

Part 3 - The edges. Write the exact sentences I say when a customer asks for something just outside the package, when they ask for a discount, and when they ask for it faster. Keep each one under 25 words and do not make promises I have not given you.

Part 4 - Migration. Tell me how to move my next three enquiries onto the package without confusing existing customers, and what to change first in how I describe my work.

Do not add services I have not told you I already deliver.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[PASTE THE FIVE]` - five recent jobs with the five details. Rough numbers are fine; the pattern matters more than precision.
- `[YOUR NUMBER OR ESTIMATE]` - your hourly cost or daily target. If you do not know it, do prompt 35 first and come back.
- `[ANYTHING YOU ALWAYS END UP DOING FOR FREE AND RESENT]` - this becomes the "not included" list and is often the most valuable part of the answer.

**Tip:** Use Part 2 as your standard quote text for the next month and keep a tally of what you had to say no to. If the same out-of-scope request comes up three times, it belongs in the package next version and the price should move with it.
