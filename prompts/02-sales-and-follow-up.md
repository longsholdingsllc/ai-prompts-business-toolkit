# Sales & Follow-Up

Selling on your own is mostly answering messages quickly and following up on the ones that went quiet, without nagging anyone. The prompts here write those messages for you and keep the follow-up honest: no invented deadlines, no pressure you cannot back up.

## 8. Reply To A New Enquiry While It Is Still Warm

**When to use:** An enquiry just landed - a form, a text, a message - and you are mid-job with no head space to write a good reply.

**Prompt:**

```text
Write my reply to a new enquiry. Keep it under 120 words, plain and human, no corporate padding.

The enquiry, copied exactly: [PASTE THE ENQUIRY]
What I sell and roughly what it costs: [YOUR OFFER AND PRICE RANGE]
What I need to know before I can quote: [THE TWO OR THREE FACTS YOU ALWAYS NEED]
My real availability: [EARLIEST DATE OR WINDOW YOU COULD ACTUALLY START]
Anything about the job that worries me: [OR "nothing"]

Give me four things:

1. The reply. It must: answer the question they actually asked, ask only the two or three facts I need (no questionnaire), state my real availability as a fact rather than a promise, and end with one clear next step.
2. A text-message version, under 45 words, for the same enquiry.
3. A list of anything in the enquiry that suggests I should decline or be careful - only if there is something real, otherwise say "nothing stands out".
4. Three questions I should ask myself before I quote this job, based on what they wrote.

Do not invent availability, discounts, certifications or waiting times I did not give you.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[PASTE THE ENQUIRY]` - the message as received, including the messy parts. The wording tells the assistant a lot about the person.
- `[YOUR OFFER AND PRICE RANGE]` - what you do and roughly what it costs, so the reply does not accidentally imply something cheaper.
- `[THE TWO OR THREE FACTS YOU ALWAYS NEED]` - the details without which you cannot price the job.
- `[EARLIEST DATE OR WINDOW YOU COULD ACTUALLY START]` - a real date. If you are unsure, say what you know.
- `[OR "nothing"]` - gut feeling about the job.

**Tip:** Save the reply as a template with the two or three facts as blanks, so the next enquiry takes two minutes rather than twenty. Send the text version when the enquiry came by text - matching their channel gets answered more often than switching to email.

## 9. Qualify It Before You Spend An Hour On It

**When to use:** You keep writing quotes that go nowhere, or taking on jobs that turn out to be a bad fit halfway through.

**Prompt:**

```text
Act as a sales filter for a one-person business. Work only from what I give you.

My business and typical job value: [WHAT YOU SELL, USUAL PRICE RANGE]
What a good job looks like for me: [THE JOBS YOU WANT MORE OF]
What a bad job looks like: [THE JOBS YOU REGRET TAKING]
The enquiry in the customer's own words: [PASTE IT]

Produce a table with one row per thing they told me. Columns:
- What they said
- What it tells me - and mark GUESS if you are inferring rather than reading
- What I should ask next to confirm it
- Green flag, amber or red flag

Then:
1. Give the enquiry a fit score out of 10 using only the evidence, and one sentence explaining the score.
2. Write the three questions I should send to fill the biggest gaps. They must be answerable in one line each by a busy person.
3. Tell me plainly whether this is worth an hour of my time, and what would have to be true for the answer to change.

Do not fill gaps with assumptions about the customer's budget or intent.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[WHAT YOU SELL, USUAL PRICE RANGE]` - so the assistant can judge whether the job is in your range.
- `[THE JOBS YOU WANT MORE OF]` and `[THE JOBS YOU REGRET TAKING]` - be honest, including the jobs you regret for reasons that are not about money.
- `[PASTE IT]` - the full enquiry, not a summary.

**Tip:** After five enquiries, look at the scores against what actually happened. If your red flags never turn into real problems, you are filtering too aggressively; if you keep accepting amber jobs that go wrong, add whatever they had in common to your "bad job" list and run this prompt again.

## 10. The Three-Message Follow-Up That Is Not Nagging

**When to use:** You sent a quote or a proposal and heard nothing back. You know you should follow up and you also do not want to be that person.

**Prompt:**

```text
Write a three-message follow-up for a quote that has gone quiet. Each message must be under 90 words, sound like a person, and add something new. No "just checking in", no fake deadlines, no "final notice", no pressure.

What I quoted: [WHAT YOU QUOTED AND THE PRICE]
When I sent it: [DATE OR "X DAYS AGO"]
What they said, if anything: [PASTE REPLIES OR "nothing"]
Anything that has genuinely changed since - my availability, a price change, a new option: [OR "nothing has changed"]
Anything I can honestly offer them instead if the full job is too much: [SMALLER OPTION OR "nothing"]

Give me:

1. Message 1, for about two days after the quote: short, useful, one question that is easy to answer.
2. Message 2, for about a week later: it must contain something new - a worked example, an option, a clarification - not a repeat of message 1.
3. Message 3, for about two weeks after that: it closes the file politely and gives them an easy, no-awkwardness way to say no.
4. For each message: the subject line if it is email, and what I should do if they do not reply.
5. One sentence per message explaining what makes it not pushy.

Do not add deadlines, discounts or urgency I have not told you exist. If nothing has genuinely changed, say so and write the messages without pretending it has.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[WHAT YOU QUOTED AND THE PRICE]` - enough detail that the messages can reference the job.
- `[DATE OR "X DAYS AGO"]` - so the timing lines up with reality.
- `[PASTE REPLIES OR "nothing"]` - any partial reply you got, even "let me think about it".
- `[OR "nothing has changed"]` and `[SMALLER OPTION OR "nothing"]` - be truthful here. If nothing has changed, the follow-up should still be fine: people are busy, not hostile.
**Note:** This follow-up is for someone who contacted you first. Do not use it to send unsolicited bulk email.

**Tip:** Diary messages 1 to 3 the moment you send a quote and stop thinking about it until each comes up. Most quotes that convert are decided by message 2, and most people never send it. If all three get no reply, move them to a list you look at once a quarter rather than chasing again.

## 11. The One-Page Proposal

**When to use:** Your quotes are long, your emails are long, and you suspect the customer skims and then does nothing.

**Prompt:**

```text
Turn my notes into a one-page proposal. One page means one page: if it does not fit, cut something rather than shrinking the text.

What they asked for, in their words: [PASTE THEIR REQUEST OR SUMMARY OF THE CALL]
What I will do, step by step: [YOUR LIST OF STEPS, IN PLAIN TERMS]
What it costs: [PRICE, AND WHAT THE PRICE INCLUDES]
Timings I can honestly commit to: [DATES OR "I cannot commit to dates yet"]
What I need from them to start: [DEPOSIT, INFO, ACCESS, DECISIONS]
What is not included: [THE SCOPE EDGE YOU ALWAYS GET ASKED FOR]
What happens if something changes mid-job: [HOW YOU HANDLE CHANGES]

Produce the proposal in this order: what you asked for, what I will do, what it costs, what I need from you, what is not included, what happens next. Use short sentences and plain headings. Keep every promise traceable to something I told you - mark anything you had to phrase as a placeholder with [CONFIRM] so I fill it in before sending.

Then give me two extra things:
1. Three lines I should delete if they are only there to make the proposal longer.
2. One question I should ask the customer before sending this, because it would change the scope or the price.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[PASTE THEIR REQUEST OR SUMMARY OF THE CALL]` - mirroring their words is what makes a proposal land.
- `[YOUR LIST OF STEPS, IN PLAIN TERMS]` - what actually happens, in order.
- `[PRICE, AND WHAT THE PRICE INCLUDES]` - one number, with inclusions named.
- `[DATES OR "I cannot commit to dates yet"]` - do not let the assistant invent a schedule you have no track record for.
- `[DEPOSIT, INFO, ACCESS, DECISIONS]` - what has to happen on their side.
- `[THE SCOPE EDGE YOU ALWAYS GET ASKED FOR]` - a line here prevents most disputes.
- `[HOW YOU HANDLE CHANGES]` - your real policy on changes and extra costs.

**Tip:** Keep one master copy and change only the four job-specific blocks each time. Re-read it as if you were the customer: if you cannot find the price and the next step in ten seconds, cut something else.

## 12. Answer "That's Too Expensive" Without Cutting Your Price

**When to use:** The price lands and the conversation stalls. Your instinct is to knock something off, and you know that usually cheapens the job.

**Prompt:**

```text
Help me respond to a price objection without discounting on the spot.

What I quoted: [WHAT YOU QUOTED, WHAT IT INCLUDES, AND THE PRICE]
What they said, exactly: [PASTE THEIR WORDS]
What I know about their situation: [ANY REAL CONTEXT - TIMING, BUDGET THEY MENTIONED, WHAT THEY COMPARED IT TO]
What my actual cost of delivering this job is: [YOUR COST OR TIME]
What I can reduce, if anything, without making the job worse: [OR "nothing"]

Give me:

1. Four different replies, each under 100 words, each using a different move:
   - acknowledge the concern without apologising for the price
   - compare the price to what the alternative really costs them
   - reduce the scope instead of the price
   - ask what they are comparing it to
2. The three questions that tell me whether this is really about money, or about trust, timing or uncertainty. Include what each answer would mean.
3. A "do not say" list of five phrases that make a one-person business sound desperate or make the price look negotiable, with a better alternative for each.
4. What to do if the honest answer is that they cannot afford it right now.

Do not suggest fake scarcity, invented discounts, or implying the price ends soon.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[WHAT YOU QUOTED, WHAT IT INCLUDES, AND THE PRICE]` - so the replies can refer to the scope rather than arguing about a number.
- `[PASTE THEIR WORDS]` - "too expensive compared to what?" is often answered in their own sentence.
- `[ANY REAL CONTEXT]` - any clue about their budget or what they compared it to.
- `[YOUR COST OR TIME]` - this is what tells you how much room you actually have.
- `[OR "nothing"]` - if nothing can be cut without harm, say so and the assistant should work with that.

**Tip:** Pick the reply that feels most like you and edit it until you could say it on the phone without reading. The strongest long-run response to a price objection is usually the scope reduction, because it keeps the price intact and gives the customer a real choice.

## 13. Wake Up A Deal That Went Cold

**When to use:** Somebody enquired in spring, it drifted, and you know there is still a real job there - you just never found a reason to make contact again.

**Prompt:**

```text
Write a one-off message to restart a conversation that went quiet.

What they wanted: [WHAT THEY ASKED ABOUT]
When we last spoke and what happened: [DATE AND WHAT WAS SAID]
What I quoted, if anything: [PRICE OR "no quote sent"]
What has genuinely changed since then: [FOR EXAMPLE: my availability, a change in my pricing, a seasonal window, a new option - or "nothing has changed"]
How cold this is: [JUST WENT QUIET / A FEW WEEKS / MONTHS AGO / MORE THAN A YEAR]

Give me three versions, each under 110 words:
1. The circumstances-changed message, for when the timing was the problem.
2. The practical nudge message, for when they simply got busy: one useful piece of information and one easy question.
3. The closing-the-file message, for the coldest cases: it politely says I am tidying up my list and asks whether to keep or close it.

Rules for all three: no fake deadlines, no "checking in", no implying demand that does not exist. Each must end with a way for them to say no that costs them nothing. Then tell me which version to use for this situation and why, and what I should do if they reply.

If nothing has genuinely changed and they went quiet after a quote, tell me the honest option is to write it off instead of chasing.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[WHAT THEY ASKED ABOUT]` and `[DATE AND WHAT WAS SAID]` - the history, briefly.
- `[PRICE OR "no quote sent"]` - whether there is a number on the table.
- `[OR "nothing has changed"]` - do not manufacture news. A truthful "nothing has changed" often produces a better message than an invented reason.
- `[JUST WENT QUIET / A FEW WEEKS / MONTHS AGO / MORE THAN A YEAR]` - sets the tone.
**Note:** Use this for people who approached you, or who you have done work for. Do not use it to mass-mail a bought list.

**Tip:** Run this on three cold deals in one sitting, send the messages, and put a note in your diary for two weeks later. Whatever comes back is usually not a sale on the first message - it is permission to have the conversation again.

## 14. Turn One Finished Job Into The Next Three

**When to use:** The job is done and paid, the customer is happy, and you move straight on with nothing to show for it and no new work booked.

**Prompt:**

```text
Help me get everything out of a job I just finished, without being pushy.

What the job was: [WHAT YOU DID]
Who they were: [CUSTOMER TYPE AND ANYTHING YOU LEARNED ABOUT THEM]
What they said when it was done: [PASTE OR PARAPHRASE THEIR FEEDBACK]
What they bought: [SCOPE AND PRICE]
What they will need next, if I am honest about it: [OR "nothing soon"]
What they could plausibly introduce me to: [TYPES OF PEOPLE OR COMPANIES, NOT NAMES OR CONTACTS]

Give me:

1. A thank-you message under 100 words that does exactly one thing - either asks for a short review, asks for one introduction, or offers the next logical piece of work. Choose the one thing based on what I told you, and say why you chose it.
2. A follow-up asking for introductions, written as a specific question rather than "please refer me": list the three or four kinds of people it makes sense to ask about, in a way they can answer with a yes or a no.
3. A check-in message to send in 60 to 90 days, based on what they will actually need next.
4. A short note for my own records: the one sentence to save about this customer so that next contact does not start from scratch.

Keep the tone like mine, not like a marketing department.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[WHAT YOU DID]` and `[SCOPE AND PRICE]` - the job, plainly.
- `[CUSTOMER TYPE AND ANYTHING YOU LEARNED ABOUT THEM]` - their situation, so the next-work suggestion is real.
- `[PASTE OR PARAPHRASE THEIR FEEDBACK]` - their words give the assistant the tone that works with this person.
- `[OR "nothing soon"]` - if there is genuinely nothing to sell them, the answer should focus on review and introductions only.
- `[TYPES OF PEOPLE OR COMPANIES, NOT NAMES OR CONTACTS]` - you decide who to actually ask; the assistant should not invent names.

**Tip:** Do the thank-you and the record note in the same ten minutes, while the job is fresh, and put the 60 to 90 day check-in in your diary immediately. Asking one specific question - "is there anyone else with the same problem as you?" - beats a generic request for referrals every time. Review requests only work if the platform you ask on allows honest reviews and you are asking a customer who actually gave you feedback.
