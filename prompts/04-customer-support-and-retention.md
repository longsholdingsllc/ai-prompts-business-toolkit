# Customer Support & Retention

For a one-person business, support is not a department - it is you, mid-job, with a message you have to answer well. These prompts cover the hard conversations: complaints, impossible requests, quiet dissatisfaction, and the policies you have been explaining verbally for years.

## 22. Answer A Complaint Before It Escalates

**When to use:** A customer is unhappy and you can feel yourself either wanting to argue or wanting to over-promise to make it stop.

**Prompt:**

```text
Help me reply to an unhappy customer. My goal is to solve the actual problem and keep a straight relationship, not to win the exchange.

What I sold or did: [WHAT THE JOB WAS]
What has gone wrong, as far as I know: [THE FACTS I HAVE]
What the customer says, copied exactly: [PASTE THEIR MESSAGE]
What I am prepared to do about it: [YOUR REAL OPTIONS - E.G. REDO IT, REFUND PART, EXPLAIN, SEND SOMEONE OUT, DO NOTHING BECAUSE I WAS RIGHT]
What I absolutely cannot do: [CONSTRAINTS - TIME, COST, CONTRACT, SAFETY]
What I would like the outcome to be: [E.G. FIX IT AND KEEP THE CUSTOMER, END THE RELATIONSHIP CLEANLY]

Give me:

1. A reply under 180 words that: acknowledges the specific problem without a generic apology, states what happens next with a real time frame, and does not admit or deny liability beyond what I have told you is true.
2. A shorter version under 70 words for a text message.
3. A list of the questions I still need answered before doing anything else, in the order I should ask them.
4. Two things I should not write in this reply, and what to write instead.
5. A one-line note on what to record about this for my own records, in case it comes up again.

Do not invent policies, compensation, guarantees or timelines. Everything you offer must come from options I gave you.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[THE FACTS I HAVE]` - what actually happened, including the parts that are your fault.
- `[PASTE THEIR MESSAGE]` - exactly as sent.
- `[YOUR REAL OPTIONS - ...]` - list them all, including "do not compensate". The assistant works within your options, so give it real ones.
- `[CONSTRAINTS - TIME, COST, CONTRACT, SAFETY]` - what rules out easy answers.
- `[E.G. FIX IT AND KEEP THE CUSTOMER, END THE RELATIONSHIP CLEANLY]` - your actual goal.

**Tip:** Send part 1 within the hour if you can, even if the fix takes a week - an acknowledged problem is easier to live with than silence. Then set a reminder to check in when you said you would, because most escalations come from the second broken promise, not the first mistake. Logging part 5 takes ten seconds and settles arguments months later.

## 23. Say No Without Losing The Customer

**When to use:** A customer has asked for something you cannot or will not do - a discount, a rush job, work outside your scope, a favour that is becoming a habit.

**Prompt:**

```text
Help me say no to a customer request, clearly and without damage.

What they asked for: [PASTE THE REQUEST]
Why I am saying no: [THE REAL REASON - CAPACITY, SCOPE, PRICE, SAFETY, ETHICS, TERMS]
What I can offer instead, if anything: [SMALLER JOB, LATER DATE, DIFFERENT APPROACH, OR "nothing"]
How important this customer is to me: [CRITICAL, IMPORTANT, MARGINAL, ONE-OFF]
How I have handled similar requests before: [OR "this is the first time"]

Give me three versions of the same no:

1. Short and warm, under 60 words: no plus one useful alternative.
2. Full and clear, under 140 words: the no, the reason stated in one sentence without over-explaining, what I can do instead, and what happens next.
3. The repeat version, for when they ask the same thing a second or third time: polite, shorter, and unmistakably final.

For each version, add a line telling me what I am risking by sending it. Then write the sentence I should use if they push back after the first no, and one sentence on what to do if the honest answer is that this customer is no longer worth keeping.

Do not invent reasons, discounts, special terms or a "next opening" I have not told you exists.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[PASTE THE REQUEST]` - their words.
- `[THE REAL REASON - ...]` - you can decide whether to state it plainly or give a shorter version; the assistant needs the truth to write either.
- `[SMALLER JOB, LATER DATE, DIFFERENT APPROACH, OR "nothing"]` - a no with an alternative usually lands better, so list anything you would genuinely do.
- `[CRITICAL, IMPORTANT, MARGINAL, ONE-OFF]` - this changes the tone a lot.
- `[OR "this is the first time"]` - patterns matter more than single events.

**Tip:** Pick version 2 the first time and version 3 if the request comes back, and use the same wording each time rather than improvising a new softer answer. If you find yourself saying no to the same customer every month, that is a scope conversation, not a series of refusals - prompt 7 is the better tool.

## 24. Check On A Customer Who Has Gone Quiet

**When to use:** A regular customer has not been in touch for longer than usual, and you would rather find out why now than discover the relationship ended six months ago.

**Prompt:**

```text
Help me reach out to a customer who has gone quiet, to find out what is really happening without sounding like I am chasing money.

Who they are and what they normally buy: [CUSTOMER AND USUAL WORK]
How often they normally come back: [EVERY MONTH / QUARTER / YEAR]
How long it has been: [HOW LONG, AND WHEN THEY LAST SPENT]
What I know about their situation: [ANY REAL CONTEXT - SEASONALITY, A STAFF CHANGE, A MOVE, A NEW MANAGER]
Anything that went wrong last time: [OR "nothing I know of"]
What I would offer if they had a problem with me: [E.G. REWORK, A CALL, AN EXPLANATION, NOTHING IF I WAS RIGHT]

Give me:

1. A short check-in message under 100 words that asks about their situation first and does not mention money, prices or "just following up".
2. A version to use if I know something went wrong: it should name the issue directly and invite them to tell me what happened.
3. A short set of three questions to ask them if they reply, designed to reveal whether this is a service problem, a budget problem, a change in circumstance, or nothing at all.
4. A note on what I should do differently if several quiet customers turn out to have the same reason.

Do not offer discounts, compensation or special deals unless I listed them, and do not imply that I am owed their business.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[CUSTOMER AND USUAL WORK]` - their pattern tells you what "gone quiet" even means.
- `[HOW LONG, AND WHEN THEY LAST SPENT]` - the gap.
- `[ANY REAL CONTEXT - ...]` - if they changed jobs or the season ended, the message changes completely.
- `[OR "nothing I know of"]` - be honest; if something went wrong, directness works much better than a cheerful check-in.
- `[E.G. REWORK, A CALL, AN EXPLANATION, NOTHING IF I WAS RIGHT]` - what you would actually do if they told you they were unhappy.

**Tip:** Run this on three quiet customers at once, once a quarter, and keep a note of the reasons. If two of them give the same reason, that is a fix worth making in your process rather than a message worth sending again. Remember these are people you have traded with, not a mailing list to work through.

## 25. Write My Rules In Plain English

**When to use:** You have explained the same refund rule, cancellation policy or scope limit four times this year, slightly differently each time, and you would like one version you can actually send.

**Prompt:**

```text
Turn my business rules into plain-English wording I can use everywhere.

My business: [WHAT YOU SELL]
The rules I actually follow, told plainly, even the awkward ones: [LIST EVERYTHING - DEPOSITS, CANCELLATION, REFUNDS, LATE PAYMENT, SCOPE CHANGES, WHO OWNS THE WORK, WHAT YOU NEED FROM THE CUSTOMER]
How I explain them today: [PASTE ANY EXISTING TEXT, OR "I improvise each time"]
When they apply: [AT QUOTING, AT BOOKING, AFTER DELIVERY]
Rules I am unsure about or want an opinion on: [OR "none"]
Anything a contract or a professional body requires me to state: [OR "nothing"]

Give me:

1. A customer-facing version of each rule in one or two sentences, in plain words, no legal phrasing, no threats. Numbered so I can reference them.
2. The same rules as a short "what you can expect from me and what I need from you" section I could put in a quote or on a website: six lines or fewer.
3. A table of the rules that are unclear, contradictory, or that I would struggle to enforce, with a question I need to answer for each.
4. The two rules that, if I stated them at quoting time, would prevent the most disputes, and I should put them in writing first.

This is not legal advice. Flag anything that sounds like it needs a professional review before I use it - do not simply write it confidently.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[LIST EVERYTHING - ...]` - the real rules, as you actually apply them, including the ones you have never written down.
- `[PASTE ANY EXISTING TEXT, OR "I improvise each time"]` - so the rewrite keeps anything that already works.
- `[AT QUOTING, AT BOOKING, AFTER DELIVERY]` - the moment matters; a refund rule told at quoting prevents a fight later.
- `[OR "none"]` and `[OR "nothing"]` - honest options.
- `[OR "none"]` for rules you are unsure about - these are usually the ones causing your disputes.

**Tip:** Put the plain-English numbered version in your quote template so customers see the rules before they pay, and keep the table from part 3 for yourself. When a dispute comes up, quote your own wording rather than inventing a new explanation under pressure. Where a rule touches money, ownership or liability, have someone qualified read it before it goes on a contract.

## 26. Build My Saved Replies Library

**When to use:** You are typing the same answers over and over - directions, opening hours, what to bring, how long a job takes - and each one takes you five minutes you do not have.

**Prompt:**

```text
Build me a library of saved replies for the messages I answer most often.

My business: [WHAT YOU SELL]
How customers reach me: [TEXT, EMAIL, DMs, PHONE]
The five to ten questions or requests I answer most often: [LIST THEM AS CUSTOMERS PHRASE THEM, INCLUDING THE ANNOYING ONES]
Facts I repeat constantly: [PRICES, OPENING HOURS, AREAS COVERED, LEAD TIMES, WHAT TO BRING OR PREPARE]
Things I must never promise: [OR "nothing"]

Produce a table with one row per saved reply and these columns: Name for the reply, When to use it, The reply text (under 80 words), The one detail I must change each time, and Any risk if I send it unedited.

Then:
1. Write the three replies in full again, with every part I must edit marked in [SQUARE BRACKETS], so an unedited send cannot go out with the wrong name, date or price.
2. Tell me which two replies to save first, and where to keep them so I will actually find them on a phone.
3. Give me one reply I did not ask for but clearly need, based on the questions I listed.

Do not invent prices, lead times or policies I have not given you.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[WHAT YOU SELL]` and `[TEXT, EMAIL, DMs, PHONE]` - channel shapes the reply length.
- `[LIST THEM AS CUSTOMERS PHRASE THEM, INCLUDING THE ANNOYING ONES]` - the annoying ones are the biggest time savers.
- `[PRICES, OPENING HOURS, AREAS COVERED, LEAD TIMES, WHAT TO BRING OR PREPARE]` - the facts you repeat.
- `[OR "nothing"]` - anything that must never be promised.

**Tip:** Save these in your phone's text shortcuts or a notes app, not in an email folder, so you can reach them from wherever you answer. Review the library every few months and delete the replies you have not used; a saved reply that is out of date is worse than writing a fresh one.

## 27. Ask For Feedback You Can Actually Use

**When to use:** You want to know what customers really think, your only tool so far is "were you happy?", and the answers have told you nothing.

**Prompt:**

```text
Write me a short feedback request that produces answers I can act on.

What I do and who I did it for: [THE JOB OR SERVICE]
When the job finished: [HOW LONG AGO]
What I am unsure about: [E.G. WHETHER THE COMMUNICATION WAS POOR, WHETHER THE PRICE FELT WORTH IT, WHETHER THEY WOULD COME BACK]
What I suspect is going wrong: [YOUR HONEST GUESS OR "I do not know"]
What I will do with the answers: [E.G. FIX MY PROCESS, STOP DOING SOMETHING, TRAIN MYSELF]
How the customer prefers to communicate: [TEXT, EMAIL, CALL]

Give me:

1. A request under 70 words that asks for something specific rather than "feedback", and makes it easy to say something negative.
2. Three questions that produce usable answers, each answerable in one or two sentences. They must ask about behaviour and specifics, not about satisfaction in general. For each question, tell me what I would learn from a good answer and from a bad one.
3. A follow-up question to ask if the answer is vague or overly polite.
4. One thing to say that makes it clear I will not argue with a negative answer.
5. A short thank-you for when they do answer, and a note on when to ask rather than never asking again.

Do not promise to act on feedback unless I told you I will, and do not invent a review request or a reward.
```

**Fill in:**

- `[THE JOB OR SERVICE]` and `[HOW LONG AGO]` - feedback is most useful soon after delivery.
- `[E.G. WHETHER THE COMMUNICATION WAS POOR, ...]` - if you already suspect the problem, aim the questions at it.
- `[YOUR HONEST GUESS OR "I do not know"]` - your suspicion makes the questions sharper.
- `[E.G. FIX MY PROCESS, STOP DOING SOMETHING, TRAIN MYSELF]` - only ask if you intend to use the answer.
- `[TEXT, EMAIL, CALL]` - a phone call gets more truth than a form.

**Tip:** Ask three customers this week, using the version that matches how each of them communicates, and write the answers down in one place before you respond to any of them. If you promise a fix in part 4, come back to that customer when it is done - that single follow-up does more for repeat business than any review request.
